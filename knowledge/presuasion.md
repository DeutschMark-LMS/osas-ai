# PRE-SUASION (ROBERT CIALDINI) — THE FRONTLOADING OF ATTENTION

> Chapter-by-chapter distillation of Cialdini's follow-up to *Influence*. *Influence* governs **what** to say; pre-suasion governs **when** to say it.

## CHAPTER 1: PRE-SUASION — AN INTRODUCTION

### THE CORE PREMISE
*   **The Finding:** When Cialdini infiltrated the training programs of salespeople, fundraisers, recruiters and advertisers, he expected the top performers to spend most of their time refining the *request* — its clarity, logic, features. They didn't. The highest achievers spent more time crafting **what they said and did immediately BEFORE the request**. "Skilled gardeners who know that even the finest seeds will not take root in stony soil."
*   **The Definition:** **Pre-suasion** = the process of arranging for recipients to be receptive to a message *before* they encounter it. The message itself can be unaltered, fixed by someone else in the organization. Only the moment is yours to engineer.
*   **The Tenet:** **What we present first changes the way people experience what we present next.** A number, a line length, a song — any first exposure primes the response to the second.
*   **Likelihood, Not Certainty:** No persuasive practice works every time. The game is played on *probability*. "A meaningful increase in those odds is enough to gain a decisive advantage" — enough to outpace rivals with equally good cases to make.
*   **The Opening Case (Jim, fire alarm sales):** Company script was fixed — timed fire-safety quiz, then demo, then the materials book. Jim's only deviation: mid-quiz he would "forget" materials in his car and ask to let himself out and back in. Twice he refused to explain; pressed, he snapped: *"Who do you let walk in and out of your house on their own? Only somebody you trust, right? I want to be associated with trust in those families' minds."* He never claimed to be trusted — he arranged to be **treated** like a trusted person. The tactic was the sole difference between him and his far less successful colleagues.
*   **Openers:** Frames, anchors, primes, mindsets, first impressions — all names for the same object. Cialdini unifies them as **openers**, because they work in two ways: (1) they *initiate* the appeal, and (2) more powerfully, they **remove existing barriers** — "openings of minds and of protectively locked doors."

### THE BIG SAME vs. THE BIG DIFFERENCE
*   **The Training Trap:** Every persuasion profession insists its arena is unique — advertising ≠ marketing ≠ fundraising ≠ PR ≠ lobbying ≠ recruitment; whole life ≠ term; wholesale ≠ retail; whole leaf ≠ chopped. Two lapses follow: detours into distinctions of no consequence, and a failure to ask the far more valuable question: **What's the same?**
*   **The Big Same:** Only **six psychological principles** showed up routinely across long-prospering influence businesses: **reciprocation, liking, social proof, authority, scarcity, consistency** (from *Influence*). If you master what works across the widest set of situations, the details of the change you want don't matter — "whole leaf or chopped."
*   **The Big Difference (this book):**
    *   *Influence* was written for **consumers** — to recognize and reject undue influence. *Pre-Suasion* is written for people who want to **wield** it.
    *   *Influence* covered what to say. This book adds **when** to say it.
    *   Persuasion is **lawful** (similar procedures produce similar results across widely varied situations), and therefore **learnable** — unlike artistic inspiration. No innate talent or phrasing gift required.
*   **Moment Monitor vs. Moment Maker:** You can *recognize* opportune moments as they naturally arise, or (more perilous, ethically) *manufacture* them. Either way, the person who knows how to time a request properly "will do exceedingly well."

## CHAPTER 2: PRIVILEGED MOMENTS
*   **The Concept:** Pre-suasive openers do not create permanent receptivity. They create a **window** — a privileged moment. The word carries two meanings: a **time-limited period** and, from physics, a **unique leveraging force** capable of unprecedented movement. That yoked temporal/physical pair has the power to instigate change in a third dimension: the psychological one.
*   **The Origin Story:** A business school associate dean promised to arrange everything Cialdini wanted — better office, more powerful computer, secretary, library, parking — then immediately asked, in the *same call*, for a last-minute favor: teach a special MBA marketing class. Cialdini had never taught in a business school, never taught marketing, never taught MBAs — and knew it would torpedo the book he was taking the leave to write. He agreed anyway: "there was no socially acceptable alternative to saying yes... **If he had asked the day before or the day after, I would have been able to say no.**" The moment, not the merits, closed the deal.
*   **The Palm-Reading Lesson (Positive Test Strategy):** Cialdini's palmistry was "almost always true" because in deciding whether a possibility is correct, **people search for hits, not misses — for confirmations, not disconfirmations**. Tell someone they're stubborn and they'll retrieve instances of stubbornness; tell the same person (two drinks later) they're flexible and they'll retrieve instances of flexibility. Both feel "absolutely right." Sherlock Holmes' edge in "Silver Blaze" was attending to what *didn't* happen (the dog that didn't bark). Most of us are sub-Holmesian.
*   **Single-Chute Questions:** Asking "unhappy or happy with your social life?" produced a **375% higher** unhappiness declaration rate than the happy-first wording. A single-chute question shunts the respondent down one engineered memory chute. *Practical implication:* refuse to answer one-sided surveys; demand two-sided ("How satisfied or dissatisfied...?"). Cult recruiters don't screen with "Are you unhappy?" — they **build** the unhappiness in the moment, then strike: "Well, if you're unhappy, you'd want to change that, right?"
*   **The Proof (Bolkan & Andersen):** Request for survey help: **29% compliance** cold. Opener: *"Do you consider yourself a helpful person?"* (nearly everyone says yes) → **77.3% compliance**. Companion study, a new soft drink sample requiring an email address: **33%** cold. Opener: *"Do you consider yourself to be somebody who is adventurous and likes to try new things?"* → **75.7%**. Of those asked, **97%** (70 of 72) called themselves adventurous. The shunted subjects even ignored the standard anti-scam caution — this was a total stranger and an email address.
*   **The Ruthlessness of Focus:** Attention is **paid** — the price is attention lost to everything else. The mind holds one thing in conscious awareness at a time; multitasking is rapid alternation. Shifting focus costs an **attentional blink** (~half a second of dead spot). What this means for influence: **whatever we focus people on seems more important to them than before.** (Milton Erickson timed crucial insights to passing trucks and then *lowered* his voice, forcing patients to lean into the information — an embodied signal of interest.) Research: shoppers who merely *envisioned moving toward* a snack came to like it more and would pay **over 4x more**.
*   **THE MAJOR THESIS:** *"Frequently the factor most likely to determine a person's choice in a situation is not the one that counsels most wisely there; it is one that has been elevated in attention (and, thereby, in privilege) at the time of the decision."*
*   **The Alternate Model of Influence:** The dominant scientific model says: to change behavior, first change a person's **beliefs, attitudes, or experiences**. The pre-suasive model says: **change nothing except what is prominent in that person's mind at the moment of decision.** The new soft drink doesn't require shifting beliefs about soft drinks — only reminding the prospect that they have historically been willing to try new things.

## CHAPTER 3: THE IMPORTANCE OF ATTENTION . . . IS IMPORTANCE
*   **The Queen's Tell:** At a Golden Jubilee receiving line, a young woman's phone rang. Elizabeth II leaned forward: *"You should answer that, dear. It might be someone important."* At that instant, no one on earth was more important than the Queen of the Realm — yet the unknown caller was adjudged worthy. **Anything that draws focused attention to itself leads observers to overestimate its importance.**
*   **The Focusing Illusion (Kahneman):** Asked for the single scientific concept that would most improve everyone's understanding of the world, Kahneman titled his answer: **"Nothing in life is as important as you think it is while you are thinking about it."** We pay attention to what matters — but the reverse also runs: whatever we attend to gets stamped as mattering.
*   **Agenda Setting (the media's real power):** The press rarely tells people **what to think**; it is "stunningly successful in telling them **what to think about**" (Bernard Cohen). Evidence:
    *   Düsseldorf train-station bombing → German concern about right-wing extremism spiked from near zero to **35%**, then fell back to near zero as coverage disappeared.
    *   9/11 tenth anniversary → citizens naming 9/11 among the two most important events of 70 years ran **~30% → 65% → back to ~30%** across the coverage arc.
    *   Rhesus monkeys will **pay** (in sacrificed food) to view high-status colony members, and demand payment to look at unimportant ones. Attention confers standing.
*   **The Flip Side — "There's no such thing as bad publicity":** Bad publicity can deflate revenue (Tiger Woods lost an estimated $22M/year post-scandal). But it also confers **raw attention, and attention anoints presumed importance**. Especially in the arts, where worth is nearly all subjective, being unforgotten beats being beloved. The fate to fear is being **ignored**.
*   **The Double-Barreled Effect:** A persuader who drags outsized attention onto the single most favorable feature of an offer wins twice — the audience (1) reasons only from the one-sided evidence they were directed toward, and (2) lends that feature **exaggerated significance before examining it.**

### BACK ROADS TO ATTENTION (three stealth tactics)
*   **A. Managing the Background:**
    *   **Clouds vs. Pennies (Mandel & Johnson):** Online sofa store. Landing-page wallpaper of **fluffy clouds** → visitors rated *comfort* as more important, searched for comfort information, and preferred the more comfortable and more expensive sofa. Wallpaper of **pennies** → cost became the priority and they chose the cheap sofa. Almost none of the participants believed the wallpaper affected them in any way.
    *   **Banner Ads:** Ads flashed for five seconds near article text — later, readers rated the brand more favorably and **never recalled seeing the ad**. And the more often a banner appeared, the more they liked it — the classic **wear-out effect never occurred**, precisely because the ads were never processed consciously. *Recognition/recall is a blind spot, not a success metric, for this format.*
    *   **Noise & clutter (real harm):** NYC children in classrooms near rattling elevated tracks scored significantly lower in reading; installing sound baffling on tracks and in classrooms **restored the scores**. Munich's airport relocation sent memory and reading scores **down** near the new site and **up** near the old. Heavily decorated classroom walls **reduce** young children's science test scores. Background information directs or distracts focus — a would-be influencer must curate it.
*   **B. Inviting Favorable Evaluation:**
    *   **Attention is not a panacea.** If the thing you spotlight is genuinely poor, you make it *more* important — and attention to a bad idea tends to **polarize** opinion against it. "Attention-capturing tactics provide no panacea."
    *   **The Unitary Advantage:** Where your case *is* strong, isolation is your friend. Ask consumers to rate your product **without** asking them to consider competitors (Canon without Nikon, Olympus, Pentax, Minolta) and ratings, purchase intent, and urgency all jump. Add comparative consideration of the rivals first and every advantage evaporates. The "How are we doing?" email is therefore not just service recovery — it is **single-chute evaluation with a hidden bonus**: favorable scrutiny with no rival scrutiny.
    *   **The Paint Manufacturer Study (managerial version):** Decision makers were asked to evaluate the wisdom of **only one** of four worthy fixes to a sales slump (more advertising, lower price, more reps, better product). Whichever one they evaluated, they then recommended as the best remedy. Why? (1) Full comparison is slow, and senior people don't get promoted for being bottlenecks; (2) **satisficing** (Herbert Simon's *satisfy + suffice*) — make it good *enough* and make it *gone*. Counter-tactic: force **"consider the opposite"** — *What future events could make this plan go wrong? What would happen to us if it did?* Companies using sound debiasing processes showed a **5–7% ROI advantage**.
*   **C. Shifting the Task at Hand (the Embedded Reporter Program):**
    *   US officials and media bureau chiefs embedded 600–700 reporters inside combat units. Reporters gained unmatched access and human-interest material (present in **>1/3** of embedded stories vs. **1%** of unembedded). The military gained favorable tilt, plus control over training, selection, dismissal, and pre-publication review. Col. Rick Long, USMC: *"Frankly, our job is to win the war. Part of that is information warfare. So we are going to attempt to dominate the information environment."*
    *   **The hidden side effect:** **93%** of embedded stories came from the soldiers' perspective, and embedded reports took **71% of front-page war coverage**. The absence of WMD — the war's core justification — was mentioned in just **2%** of all stories. The assignment had been made **molecular instead of molar**.
    *   **The lesson:** attention on *conduct* pushed *wisdom* out of frame. "The predominant media message to the public was evident: the thing you should be paying attention to here is the conduct of the war, not the wisdom of it." Since frontline execution was the campaign's strength and its justification its weakness, the focusing illusion awarded center stage to the success and buried the failure. **Change the task someone is on, and you change what they consider important — without ever arguing the point.**

## CHAPTER 4: WHAT'S FOCAL IS CAUSAL
*   **The Second Mechanic:** Focus does two things. It makes the focal element seem **more important** (Ch. 3) and it makes it seem **more causal** — because we normally direct attention to the true causes around us, seeing ourselves attend to something leads us to assume it caused what followed. Directed attention therefore grants a focal element standing in answer to the most essential human question: **Why?**
*   **The Line-Cutting Study (Oberholzer-Gee):** Offered money to cut in line — $1 → 50% let him in; $3 → 65%; $5 → 75%; $10 → 76%. Classical economics says the incentive bought compliance. But **almost no one took the money.** The real cause was the **norm of social responsibility**: the obligation to aid those in need, in proportion to their need. Higher offers signaled a *greater need*, not a better deal — "If this guy is willing to pay a lot to jump ahead of me, he must really need to get to the front fast." The following swap ("for the favor alone") then produced the *highest* compliance.
*   **The Structural Audit:** *"Merely because they are so visible (and, therefore, prominent in attention), [fiscal factors] are often less determining than they seem. Conversely, there are many other factors — social obligations, personal values, moral standards — that, merely because they are not readily observable, are often more determining than they seem."* Money doesn't just appear more important; it appears more **causal**.
*   **The Tylenol Lottery Case:** After the 1982 Chicago Tylenol cyanide poisonings (7 dead), Johnson & Johnson published production lot numbers. Lots **2,880 and 1,910** became focal through pure publicity — ordinary, unmemorable digits, grotesquely associated with poison-fed death. Yet thousands of lottery players bet on them at unprecedented rates; Rhode Island, New Hampshire, and Pennsylvania had to **suspend wagers** on the numbers because betting blew past maximum liability levels. What's focal is presumed causal — and every one of those bets lost. The lesson generalizes: "The tendency to presume that what is focal is causal holds sway too deeply, too automatically, and over too many types of human judgment."
*   **The Camera-Angle Experiments (Shelley Taylor):** Two observers watch the same scripted, deliberately balanced conversation. One sees it over the left participant's shoulder — whichever face is **more visible** is judged the more influential, the more causal. Taylor caught herself doing it in rehearsal before a single data point existed. The effect was "practically unmovable" and "automatic": it survived personally important topics, distraction, long delays, expected reporting to others, male and female judges, and live vs. videotaped delivery.
*   **The Confession Problem (Lassiter):** Interrogations are increasingly videotaped — but the camera almost always sits **behind the interrogator, aimed at the suspect's face**. So the suspect is the salient party, and observers assign the suspect greater responsibility for the confession and greater guilt. This held across men and women, students and jury-eligible adults, one or two viewings, deep or shallow thinkers, law enforcement personnel, and **criminal court judges** — and it held even when observers were explicitly warned about the bias. *The only thing that removed the bias was changing the camera angle*: a side view equalizes salience and the bias disappears; reverse the angle onto the interrogator's face and the bias **reverses** (the interrogator is now seen as having coerced the confession).
*   **The Peter Reilly Case (1973, Connecticut):** An 18-year-old returned home to find his mother dying of a savage murder. Voluntarily waived counsel because he believed the truth would free him. Sixteen hours of rotating interrogators, a polygraph operator who told him the machine proved he killed her, and a false claim of physical evidence. Pressed to "dig, dig, dig" for a repressed memory, he gradually constructed the memory from his interrogators' proposals. *"You say it, then I imagine I'm doing it."* He signed a confession that conformed to their theory and believed none of it at the outset. He was convicted; two years later, evidence in the deceased prosecutor's files proved his innocence and all charges were dropped. Broader data: of **125 false-confession cases**, suspects who renounced and pled not guilty were **still convicted 81% of the time**. Justice Brennan: *"the real trial, for all purposes, occurs as the confession is obtained."*
*   **The Arthur Miller Connection:** Miller, blacklisted by HUAC, wrote *The Crucible* (1692 Salem) as an allegory of loaded committee questioning. Nien Cheng, imprisoned during China's Cultural Revolution, told him that the questions asked of the accused in *The Crucible* "were exactly the same as the questions I had been asked by the Cultural Revolutionaries." Miller's reply: he took them from the Salem trial record. He later saw the same uncanny match in the Reilly interrogation transcript. Certain highly effective self-incriminating question sequences have been independently invented and reused across centuries and continents.
*   **The Practical Defense (memorize this):** If you're ever a person of interest in a serious investigation and the session begins to feel like an interrogation rather than information-gathering — **find the camera (usually above and behind the officer) and move your chair so the recording depicts your face and the questioner's face equally.** Don't let the what's-focal-is-presumed-causal effect disadvantage you at trial. And if you end the session and request counsel, you may say a book advised you that extensive police questioning is unsafe even for the innocent. (Cialdini's own aside: they'll never win a conviction — "I'll just find the camera and move my chair.")
*   **The Romance of Leadership:** Leaders are assigned far more causal weight than they deserve in the success or failure of the teams and organizations they head. Analysts find workforce quality, internal systems, and market conditions affect corporate profits **more** than CEO actions — yet the CEO is credited or blamed anyway. The average employee in a large corporation is paid a fraction of a percent of the CEO's pay. The cleanest explanation isn't fairness or economics — it's that the person at the top is **visually prominent, psychologically salient, and therefore assigned an unduly causal role.**

## CHAPTER 5: COMMANDERS OF ATTENTION 1 — THE ATTRACTORS
*   **The Setup:** If elevated attention delivers pre-suasive leverage, some features of information command attention automatically — no communicator effort required. Those most powerful are linked to **survival**: the sexual (reproduce) and the threatening (avoid harm). Life and death, literally. (An acquisitions editor once proposed retitling *Influence* as *Weapons of Social Seduction* so browsers "would register both sex and violence in the same glance.")
*   **Sexual — and Its Condition:**
    *   **Valentine Street:** An attractive 19-year-old asked middle-aged men to retrieve her "stolen" phone from four young toughs — a hazardous request. Only **20%** complied. But men previously asked for directions to **"Valentine Street"** (vs. "Martin Street") were nearly **twice as likely** to wade in. The sexual concept had to be activated *first*; a pre-suasive opener was required.
    *   **Sex sells selectively.** In *Advertising Age*'s top 100 campaigns of the 20th century, only **8** used sexuality in copy or imagery. Sex works for products bought for sexually related purposes (cosmetics, body scents, form-fitting clothing) and fails for soft drinks, detergents, appliances.
    *   **The Goal Gate (key nuance):** Physically attractive others only capture extra attention from people who are **in the market** for a romantic/sexual relationship. Same photos, same good looks — no effect on those not looking. **Stimuli that fit the goal a person has for that situation command the attention.**
    *   **The Relationship Barometer:** Surveyed college students in relationships. Two months later, the best predictor of a breakup was **not** how much love they felt, how satisfied they were, or how long they wanted it to last. It was **how much they had been regularly aware of and attentive to attractive alternatives**. ("The menu" — being on a diet doesn't make reading it harmless.)
    *   **The Evolution Logic (Griskevicius):** Threat pushes humans to **join the group** (safety in numbers); sexual opportunity pushes humans to **stand apart from the pack** (be the prime recipient of romantic consideration). These map exactly onto the two oldest commercial appeals: "Don't be left out" vs. "Be one of the few."
*   **Threatening:**
    *   **Dread Risks:** Risky steps taken to avoid something that is actually *less* risky, simply because it holds attention. After 9/11, long-distance travelers fled the dread of the skies for the roads — the **fatality rate for highway travel is much higher**. Roughly **1,600 Americans** died in extra auto accidents as a direct result: **six times** the number killed in the only US commercial plane crash that next year. Same pattern in London after the July 2005 subway bombings — travelers shifted to bicycles, which are more hazardous than the Underground, and travel injuries spiked for months. **Dread risks are risky.**
    *   **Fear Appeals Work — With a Condition:** Frightening messages outperform mild ones and positive-framing; large scary images on cigarette packs reliably reduce smoking across countries. But high fear can trigger **denial** ("my grandfather smoked to 80"; "cures will exist by then"). The fix: pair the chilling message with **clear information about legitimate, available steps to take**. Dutch hypoglycemia study: alarming personal risk news **plus** a diet workshop invitation → those recipients were **4x more likely** to sign up then and there, using a new belief (the workshop will help) rather than denial to manage the anxiety. *Sequence rule: don't convey the frightening fact until you can fold in accessible assistance — programs, workshops, sites, hotlines.*
    *   **The Museum Ad Experiment:** Watching a **violent** film → an ad stressing popularity ("Visited by over a million people each year") **supercharged** favorability toward the San Francisco Museum of Modern Art. Watching a **romantic** film → the identical popularity ad **deflated** attraction. A slightly altered ad stressing distinctiveness ("Stand out from the crowd") flipped the results exactly: it soared with the romantic audience and flopped with the violent one. Replicated with restaurant and Las Vegas vacation ads.
    *   **The Media-Placement Implication (a genuinely overlooked lever):** TV and radio "log editors" rigorously avoid running competitor ads back-to-back — yet nobody schedules **by programming content**. Ford buying "America's largest-selling truck for thirty-nine years" slots should favor crime dramas, horror, and news and shun rom-coms; Ford buying the F-150 distinctive FX Appearance Package ("Get ready to stand out!") should do the precise opposite. "Too bad for Ford."
*   **The Different (Change-O, Presto):**
    *   **Pavlov's Interrupted Demonstrations:** The demonstrations of classical conditioning kept failing when visitors arrived or when Pavlov himself entered. The dogs' attention was hijacked by the **novel stimulus** — new people in the room. Pavlov named it the **investigatory reflex**: "in order to survive, any animal needs to be acutely aware of immediate changes to its environment... So forceful is this reflex that it supersedes all other operations." Modern name: the **orienting response**, measurable in respiration, blood flow, skin moisture, heart rate, and a brainwave pattern (the "O-wave").
    *   **Doorway Forgetting:** Walking through a doorway disrupts memory of your purpose because the abrupt change in surroundings redirects attention to the new setting. (Comforting: "it wasn't you; it was the damned doorway.")
    *   **Shots and Cuts (the producer's craft):** A veteran TV ad producer and documentary filmmaker insisted a persuasion-oriented director must care principally about **shots and cuts** — everything else is refinement. *"You use your cuts to get people to swing attention to the parts of your message you really want them to focus on."* A cut toward your strongest feature instigates an orienting response **before the audience even experiences it**. Reality: TV advertisers instead raised scene-shift frequency **over 50%** indiscriminately — producing more total attention but **less memory for the persuasive claims and less persuasion**. Viewers' focus is scattered across relevant and irrelevant attributes. "Death by a thousand cuts."
    *   **Novelty in Static Media:** Print, billboards, email can't use cuts — so insert **novelty**: anything designed to appear distinctive, original, unfamiliar, or surprising. As long as the spotlighted item has worth, its allure leapfrogs equally or more worthy rivals.
    *   **The Sofa Study (Northwestern) — the strongest practical example:** Dream vs. Titan, comparable except cushions: Dream's were softer/more comfortable but less durable. Head-to-head: **Titan wins 58% to 42%** (durability beats comfort). Add **three weak competitor sofas — all with durable cushions like the Titan** — and the **Dream vaults to 77%** of preferred choices. Adding rivals increased the Dream's win share. Why? The three added sofas made the Dream **stand out as distinct on cushion softness and comfort** — and distinctiveness swings attention to the distinguishing factor, which elevates its perceived importance. Cialdini notes that the great majority of published persuasion science goes entirely unused by practitioners — including this finding.

## CHAPTER 6: COMMANDERS OF ATTENTION 2 — THE MAGNETIZERS
*   **The Distinction:** Drawing attention is only half the game. **Holding** it is the other half. A communicator who fastens an audience's focus on the favorable elements of an argument raises the chance the argument goes **unchallenged** — opposing points are locked out of the attentional environment. Certain information combines pulling power with **staying power**: the self-relevant, the unfinished, and the mysterious.
*   **The Self-Relevant (the superglue of attention):**
    *   **The Group Photo Test:** Pass a group photo around. Every person looks **first, longest, and last at themselves.** Information about the self is an electromagnetic magnet for attention.
    *   **Tailored Health Messages:** Messages customized to the recipient (referencing their age, sex, or health history) are more attended to, found more interesting, taken more seriously, remembered better, and saved for future reference — improving outcomes across weight loss, exercise initiation, smoking cessation, and cancer screening. With digitized records and mobile devices, personalization is now cheap. "Any health communicator who has not fully investigated the potential use of such tools should be embarrassed."
    *   **The "You" Switch (copywriting application):** Ad copy for a superior antiperspirant opened: *"After all these years, people might accept that antiperspirants just aren't gonna get any better. They might even accept the ugly stains... They won't have to anymore."* Change the externalizing words **people** and **they** to the personalizing pronoun **you** and attitude toward the product improves.
    *   **THE CRITICAL CAVEAT:** *Self-relevance brings attention, not automatic approval.* If your case is strong, the "you" version wins bigger. If the rest of the ad provides weak evidence, the personalized opener **backfires** — you've made an attentive audience evaluate a feeble case. **A self-relevant opener requires a strong case behind it.**
    *   **The Next-in-Line Effect:** Cialdini sat front row for a Balanchine/Stravinsky performance by Edward Villella minutes before his own talk — and **didn't see a single moment of it**, because he was rehearsing his own speech. Same in meetings: a person speaking immediately before or after you will likely miss your content, because they are internally rehearsing or internally rehashing. **Tactical inference:** if you have a strong solution and want the most influential person at the table to absorb it, don't sit next to them. Sit **across** from them — (1) they'll be far enough from their own turn to process yours, and (2) your visual prominence makes you seem more responsible for the insight (the what's-focal-is-causal effect). And if your solution is *weak*? Sit right beside them, inside their self-focus bubble.
*   **The Unfinished (the Zeigarnik Effect):**
    *   **The Berlin Beer Garden (Lewin & Zeigarnik):** A veteran waiter could perfectly remember and distribute large tables' orders — with no written record. Asked to recall the group's orders after they covered their plates, he couldn't come close. Once the task of serving was **completed**, it stopped hoarding attention. **Unfinished tasks are the more memorable; completed ones release attention for other pursuits.**
    *   **Two conclusions from 600+ studies:** (1) On a task we feel committed to performing, we remember all sorts of elements of it **better if we haven't finished** — attention remains drawn to it. (2) Being interrupted triggers a **discomforting, gnawing desire to get back to it** — a craving for **cognitive closure** that also pulls us toward incomplete narratives, unresolved problems, unanswered questions, and unachieved goals.
    *   **Proof — Unfinished Ads:** People watched or listened to TV programming with commercials for soft drinks, mouthwash, and pain relievers. **Greatest recall** went to ads the researchers stopped **five to six seconds before their natural endings** — and the advantage was evident immediately, two days later, and **especially two weeks later**.
    *   **Proof — Uncertain Romantic Ratings:** College women whose Facebook profiles were rated by attractive men preferred, later, **not** the men who rated them highest — but the men whose ratings **remained unknown**. When an important outcome is unknown, "they can hardly think of anything else." Repeated refocusing made those men seem the most attractive.
    *   **The Writing Productivity Secret:** Maugham: *"I enjoy having written."* Most writers stall because the finish is the painful part. One prolific colleague's rule: **never end a writing session at the end of a paragraph or even a thought.** She knows exactly what she wants to say next — she just refuses to say it until the next session. This keeps the final feature permanently **near-finished**, weaponizing the drive for closure to drag her back to the chair impatient to write. (Generalized: end the day mid-thought, not at a clean stopping point.)
*   **The Mysterious:**
    *   **Einstein's Naming:** The mysterious is simultaneously "the most beautiful thing we can experience" and "the source of all true science and art."
    *   **The Discovery Method:** Studying academic books written for general audiences, Cialdini found the standard failures (unclear, stilted, jargon) and successes (clarity, vivid examples, humor, logic) — plus one unanticipated trait of the **best** pieces: **each began with a mystery story** — a perplexing state of affairs offered as an invitation to read on and dispatch the enigma. (And he'd never noticed it before *because the device worked*: "mysteries are so compelling that the reader can't remain an aloof outside observer of story structure.")
    *   **The Classroom Accident:** Cialdini mis-timed a lecture and the bell rang before he resolved his opening puzzle. Usually, five minutes before a period ends, students pack up. This time **nobody moved**, and when he tried to stop they pelted him with protests until he gave them closure. **"Cialdini, you've stumbled onto dynamite here."**
    *   **Why Mysteries Beat Descriptions and Questions:** Descriptions require **notice**. Questions require **answers**. Mysteries require **explanations.** Challenging an audience to explain puzzling states of affairs produced **higher test scores**, because explanation is the route to meaningful, enduring understanding.
    *   **The Six-Step Mystery Structure (the master teaching template):**
        1.  **Pose the Mystery.** A strange outcome nobody expects. *(Tobacco consumption had slid 10% by the late 1960s — then Big Tobacco did something that ended the decline and boosted consumption **while cutting advertising spend by a third**. What was it?)*
        2.  **Deepen the Mystery.** *(On July 22, 1969, the major US tobacco companies strongly advocated a proposal to ban all their own TV and radio ads — even though broadcast was the most effective route to new sales. And it happened.)*
        3.  **Home In on the Proper Explanation by Considering and Killing Alternatives.** *(Was it corporate conscience about the 1964 Surgeon General's report? No — the broadcast industry sued to overturn the law; only tobacco supported it. Did they stop pushing sales? No — they merely moved to print, sponsorships, giveaways, and 22 paid film placements in four years.)*
        4.  **Provide a Clue.** *(What was special about broadcast? The FCC's 1967 fairness doctrine required equal free airtime to opposing sides of controversial issues — on radio and TV only.)*
        5.  **Resolve the Mystery.** *(Anti-tobacco forces could finally afford counter-ads, which cut consumption nearly 10% over three years. Every paid tobacco ad bought a free counter-ad attacking it. So the industry banned its own broadcast ads, killing the free counter-advertising — sales jumped and ad spend fell.)*
        6.  **Draw the Implication.** *(One of the best ways to enhance acceptance of your message is to **reduce the availability of strong counterarguments** — because counterarguments are typically more powerful than arguments.)*
    *   **The Bonus of the Form:** A mystery doesn't just hold general focus — it makes people **want the details**, the necessary but often boring and attention-deflecting particulars. "What more could a communicator with a strong but intricate case want?" (Instructional note from Cialdini: don't preach the sequence from a pulpit — invite the audience into the discovery, let them offer speculations and explanations, and at the end ask if they can construct an alternative explanation that also fits all the evidence.)

## CHAPTER 7: THE PRIMACY OF ASSOCIATIONS — I LINK, THEREFORE I THINK
*   **The Foundation:** *"In the family of ideas, there are no orphans."* Every notion exists in a network of relatives linked by shared associations. Associations are the **building blocks of thought** — the way amino acids are the building blocks of life. Therefore: **all mental activity arises as patterns of associations, and influence attempts succeed only to the extent that the associations they trigger are favorable to change.**
*   **A Correction to the Standard Advice:** Trainers say: to convince others, use language that manages their **thoughts, perceptions, or emotional reactions**. Partially right. We convince others by using language that manages their **mental associations** to our message — their thoughts, perceptions, and emotions merely **proceed from** those associations.
*   **Language's Real Job (Semin's psycholinguistics):** *The main purpose of speech is to direct listeners' attention to a selected sector of reality.* Once there, the listeners' **existing associations** to the spotlighted sector take over and determine the reaction. So language is not primarily a mechanism of **conveyance** (delivering your conception of reality) — it is primarily a mechanism of **influence** (inducing recipients to share that conception, or at least act in accord with it). When we describe a film we liked, we're not explaining our position — we're **persuading**.
*   **The Linguistic Devices that Aim Attention:** verbs that draw focus to concrete features of a situation; adjectives that pull focus onto others' **traits** (rather than behaviors); personal pronouns that highlight existing relationships; metaphors that frame a state of affairs so it's interpreted one way; and simply **particular wordings** that link to targeted thoughts.
*   **Case Study — SSM Health's Weaponized Vocabulary:** At this not-for-profit hospital system's leadership conference, the speaker was told: no bullet points, and don't tell us how to "attack" our influence problems. Clarified: *"As a health care organization, we're devoted to acts of healing, so we never use language associated with violence. We don't have bullet points; we have **information points**. We don't **attack** a problem, we **approach** it."* A physician added: *"We've replaced business **targets** with business **goals**. And one of those goals is no longer to **beat** our competition; it's to **outdistance or outpace** them."* Cialdini was initially skeptical — "Geez, this is silly." He became a convert after reviewing the research: **"Geez, this is smart."**
    *   *Key nuance:* SSM did **not** eliminate achievement language — it swapped *menacing* achievement words (target, beat) for *comparable non-menacing* ones (goal, outdistance). Violence-laden language leads to elevated harm-doing and should be eliminated; achievement-laden language leads to elevated performance and should be **retained**.
*   **Incidental Exposure to Words (the evidence):**
    *   **Hostile Words → Aggression:** Participants unscrambled thirty sets of scrambled words. One group's correct sentences were mostly aggression-related (*"hit he them" → "he hit them"*). Then they delivered twenty electric shocks to a fellow subject and chose the intensity. Prior exposure to violence-linked words produced a **48% jump in selected shock intensity**. Same effect appears with **subliminal** presentation — subjects unaware they encountered violent language became significantly more aggressive.
    *   **Achievement Words → Performance:** Subtle exposure to words connoting achievement (win, attain, succeed, master) increases performance on an assigned task and **more than doubles** willingness to keep working at it. (Which rehabilitates those single-word call-center posters: OVERCOME, SUCCEED, PERSEVERE, ACHIEVE.)
    *   **The Runner Photo (Canadian call center):** Fundraisers soliciting for a local university received identical information at the start of their shifts. Half got it on plain paper; half got it printed alongside a **photo of a runner winning a race** (previously shown to activate achievement thinking). By the end of their three-hour shifts, the second group had raised **60% more money**. And a follow-up found ongoing exposure to the achievement photo produced greater success on **each of four consecutive days**. (A related finding: on a task requiring thoughtful assessment, exposure to Rodin's *The Thinker* produced a **48% increase in correct decisions**.)
    *   *Conrad:* "He who wants to persuade should put his trust not in the right argument, but in the right word."
*   **Metaphor Is a Meta-Door (to Change):** *"If you want to change the world, change the metaphor."* (Campbell)
    *   **What metaphor does:** It directs people to think of one thing **in terms of their associations to a selected other thing**. Associations transfer.
    *   **The Hidden Strategy in "Hitting the Wall":** runners describe motoric collapse as a wall — a barrier that blocks passage, can't be dispatched, can't be denied. But a physical wall normally owes its presence to **someone other than the person who hits it**, whereas a runner's wall owes its presence to the runner (training, pacing). Choosing the wall metaphor may be a chosen frame to depict the failing as **external, not of their doing, and not their fault.**
    *   **The Crime Study (Stanford) — the cleanest demonstration:** Same news account of a three-year rise in city crime rates, with **one word changed**: crime portrayed as a **ravaging BEAST** vs. a **ravaging VIRUS**. Readers who saw "beast" preferred **catch-and-cage** solutions; readers who saw "virus" preferred **remove-unhealthy-conditions** solutions. The difference caused by that single word — **22%** — was **more than double** the differences produced by readers' gender (9%) or political party affiliation (8%). *"Rarely... do [campaigns] consider the potentially greater predictive power of a pre-suasively deployed metaphor."*
    *   **The Consultant's Advice Rule:** Know the shape of the solution you want endorsed, then choose the metaphor whose **natural handling** is that solution. Beast → catch and cage. Virus → remove the conditions that let it breed. The audience derives the policy from the metaphor without being told.
    *   **Ben Feldman (the greatest life insurance salesman of his time):** Operating out of East Liverpool, Ohio with a 60-mile radius, at his peak he sold more life insurance by himself than 1,500 of the 1,800 US insurance agencies. In 1992, hospitalized with a cerebral hemorrhage at 80, he won "Feldman February" — a company-wide contest — closing **$15 million in new contracts in 28 days from his hospital bed.** His mechanism was metaphor, not pressure: people didn't **die**, they **"walked out"** of life (a breach in family responsibility that must be filled) — and *"when you walk out, your insurance money walks in."*
    *   **Metaphor Fires Without a Word:** Weight is metaphorically linked to seriousness, importance, and effort. Therefore: (1) raters reading a job candidate's qualifications **on a heavy clipboard** see a more serious contender; (2) raters reading a report on a heavy clipboard see the topic as **more important**; (3) raters holding a heavy object (requiring more effort) put **more effort** into considering a city improvement project's pros and cons. *Implication:* the drive to make e-readers as light as possible may cheapen perceived value, author depth, and reader effort. Warmth is likewise linked to personal warmth — briefly holding a **hot** (vs. iced) coffee makes people feel warmer toward, closer to, and more trusting of those around them, and more giving and cooperative afterward. **Powerful metaphoric associations can be pre-suasively activated without a single word; touch is enough.**
*   **Negative Associations Transfer Equally (Brand Safety):**
    *   A white official resigned after using the word **niggardly** ("miserly") — a different family of associations was provoked.
    *   Used-car salespeople are taught to say **"preowned"** (possession, ownership), never "used" (wear and tear).
    *   IT providers are counseled to avoid "cost" and "price" (loss of resources) in favor of **"purchase" or "investment"** (gain).
    *   Airline crew training avoids death-related language: "your **final** destination" → "your destination"; **"terminal" → "gate."**
    *   **The single most damaging negatively-loaded concept** in human evaluation — the one that poisons immediate assessments and future dealings — is **untrustworthiness**, along with its concomitants: lying and cheating.
*   **The Self-Connection Effect (Implicit Egoism):** The concept with the most favorable impact on evaluation is **the self** — because it both (a) draws and holds attention with electromagnetic strength, and (b) arrives pre-loaded with the overwhelmingly positive associations the vast majority of us shower on ourselves. So **anything self-connected — or made to seem self-connected — gets an immediate lift.**
    *   People who learn they share a **birthday, birthplace, or first name** come to like each other more, and become more cooperative and helpful. Potential customers are more willing to enroll in an exercise program if told they share a **date of birth** with the trainer. Young women are **twice as likely** to "friend" a man on Facebook if he claims the same birthday. Microfinance loans are significantly more likely to be offered to recipients whose **names share the lender's initials**. People prefer products — crackers, chocolates, teas — whose **names share letters** with their own names.
    *   **Coca-Cola's "Share a Coke":** In summer 2013, Coca-Cola's UK division replaced its own package branding with one of **150 of the most common first names** in the UK — on **100 million packs**. Justification: identical programs in Australia and New Zealand had significantly boosted sales the year before, and in the US it produced the **first increase in Coke sales in a decade**.
    *   **Even Organizations Succumb:** *Rolling Stone*'s 2004 list of the 500 greatest rock songs put "Like a **Rolling** Stone" and "(I Can't Get No) Satisfaction" by the **Rolling Stones** at #1 and #2. Cialdini checked ten comparable lists; none ranked either song first or second.
*   **"I Am We, and We Are Number One" (the social self):** The overvalued self need not be the individual self — it can be the **social self**, framed by the characteristics of the individual's group. In non-Western societies the self is particularly likely to reside outside the individual and inside the collective.
    *   **The Ad Analysis:** Two years of magazine ads in the US and South Korea — Korean ads linked products to the reader's **family or group**; American ads linked to the **individual**. Group-linked ads were more effective in Korea; individual-linked ads were more effective in the US.
    *   **The Afghan Hostage Negotiation (the most impressive case in the book):** In July 2007 the Afghan Taliban kidnapped 21 South Korean church-sponsored aid workers, killing two, with talks so bad the kidnappers had named the next two to be murdered. Kim Man-bok, head of South Korea's National Intelligence Service, flew in with a plan: he replaced his head negotiator — whose appeals went through an Afghan translator — with a South Korean representative who **spoke fluent Pashtun**. According to Kim: *"The key in the negotiations was language."* Not for precision or lucidity — for something more primitive and pre-suasive. *"When our counterparts saw that our negotiator was speaking their language, Pashtun, they developed a kind of strong intimacy with us, and so the talks went well."* The hostages were released swiftly. (Note the definitional subtlety: speaking their language is not similarity via the **liking** principle; it is a **unity** move — a cue of shared identity.)
*   **"Easy" Does It (Cognitive Fluency):**
    *   **Fluency = truth and value.** When we can picture or process something quickly and effortlessly, we not only like it more — we judge it **more valid and worthwhile.**
    *   **Rhyme → perceived truth:** "Caution and measure will win you riches" is judged more true as **"Caution and measure win you treasure."** *"To make it climb, make it rhyme."* Rhyme and regular meter also evoke higher perceptions of aesthetic value — the opposite of what free-verse advocates and modern poetry gatekeepers appear to believe.
    *   **Faces and names:** We have greater liking for faces that are easy to recognize and names that are **easy to pronounce.** Fluent processing produces increased activity in the zygomaticus major — the muscles that produce a smile.
    *   **The Law Firm Data:** Across 500 attorneys at ten US law firms, **the harder an attorney's name was to pronounce, the lower he or she stayed in the firm's hierarchy** — and this held independent of foreignness (a hard-to-pronounce foreign name fares worse than an easy-to-pronounce foreign name).
    *   **Product names:** Hard-to-pronounce drugs and food additives are rated less favorably, including on potential risks. (Why do pharmaceutical firms name products Xeljanz and Farxiga?)
    *   **Restaurant menus:** Food described in difficult-to-process copy (illegible flourish-filled fonts, bad light) is judged **less tempting** — and difficult-to-read claims in general are judged **less true**.
    *   **Stock Exchanges (the commercial consequence):** Of 89 randomly selected companies that began trading on the NYSE between 1990 and 2004, those with **easier-to-pronounce names outperformed** those with difficult names (the effect dwindled over time). Same result on the American Stock Exchange for easy-to-pronounce three-letter ticker codes (e.g., **KAR**) versus difficult ones (e.g., **RDO**).
    *   **The Counter-Note (disfluency has a use):** Godzilla vs. Mothra. When the same content is rendered in a **harder** form — smaller type, lower contrast — there is no inherent benefit. But difficulty **signals the need to think**, and in a persuasion setting that can **increase** issue-relevant thought — the route to strong, lasting attitudes. The practical reading: **when the case is hard, serious, and compelling, difficulty of processing is not automatically harmful.**
## CHAPTER 8: PERSUASIVE GEOGRAPHIES — ALL THE RIGHT PLACES, ALL THE RIGHT TRACES
*   **The Core Claim:** *There is a geography of influence.* Words and images prompt favorable associations — so do **places**. You can move yourself toward desired outcomes by locating to physical and psychological environments **prefit with cues associated with your goal**, and you can move others by shifting them into cue-rich environments.
*   **The Two Desks (Cialdini's own discovery):** Writing his first general-audience book, he had a desk at his temporary university office (shelves of professional books, journals, articles; view of academic institutes) and a desk at his rented apartment (newspapers, magazines, a view of pedestrians going about ordinary life). A month in, the pages written **at home** were "miles better" for the general audience — style and structure that had been written at the university desk were "poorly suited to anyone but professional colleagues." He'd known his target market all along; there were simply **no cues in that environment to spur him to think routinely and automatically of those readers.** The fix: revise everything at home; route popular writing home and colleague-targeted work to the office. *(Proof of the change: the book's opening line went from "My academic subdiscipline, experimental social psychology, has as a principal domain the study of the social influence process" to "I can admit it freely now: all my life I've been a patsy.")*
*   **Altering External Geography (the incentive-program consultancy):** A project manager building employee incentive programs reported that her best work happened in one odd kind of space at client headquarters: **centrally located rooms with glass walls**. She'd expected the opposite — all those employees swirling by should distract. Cialdini's explanation: to design programs for employees, the team needed **ongoing visual exposure to the people the programs would cover**. She said she couldn't choose her working space — that was the client's prerogative, and most buildings don't even have a glass-walled conference room.
    *   **The Workaround (worth copying):** Her youngest staffer's solution — **before traveling to any working meeting, download photos of program-eligible employees from the client's website and internal publications, enlarge them, mount them on poster boards, and lean them against the walls of whatever conference room they're given.** Clients loved it as "the personalized touch." The team refined it: **action shots of employees at work outperform headshots.** They were pre-suading **themselves**, and they refused to be disadvantaged by a suboptimal environment.
    *   *Supporting research:* showing radiologists a photograph of a patient alongside the X-ray increased the length of their reports and the extent to which they conscientiously registered every clinically significant aspect of the image.
*   **Two Options:** (a) alter key components of your **external** self-persuasive geography, or (b) alter key components of your **internal** one.
*   **What's Already in Us (Internal Geography):**
    *   **Contagion of coughs and symptoms:** One cougher begins and the house descends into bedlam; a dinner of 200 editorial writers was evacuated (including Attorney General Janet Reno) by a spreading coughing fit with **no physical cause found**. Other documented clusters: Austria, spider-bite reports → residents flooded hospitals, the wrong outnumbering the right by **4,000%**; a Tennessee classroom gas smell → **100 people to ERs that day, 71 more when school reopened five days later**, no leak ever found; two Canadian oil-refinery towns told of 25% higher cancer rates → residents perceived a wave of toxic-exposure health problems, then the study was **retracted for a statistical miscalculation**; a German lecture on itchy dermatological conditions → the audience **scratched themselves at an increased rate**.
    *   **Medical Student Syndrome:** **70–80%** of medical students experience the symptoms of whatever disease they're studying and become convinced they have it. Warnings don't help; experienced serially with each "disease of the week," the symptoms feel alarmingly real. Dr. George Lincoln Walton, 1908: *"The knowledge that pneumonia produces pain in a certain spot leads to a **concentration of attention upon that region**, which causes any sensation there to give alarm. The mere knowledge of the location of the appendix transforms the most harmless sensations in that region into symptoms of serious menace."*
    *   **The Self-Influence Implication:** Lying in low-level wait within each of us are **units of experience** that can be given sudden standing and force by diverting attention to them. The constituents of a cough exist in everyone — concentrate on the upper half of the lungs, where coughs start, and you can activate them. Same for dizziness (middle of the brain), nausea (top of the stomach), headache (just above the eyes). **But those waiting units also include advantageous attitudes, productive traits, and useful capacities — which we can energize by channeling attention to them instead.**
*   **The Positivity Paradox — and the Elderly Solution:**
    *   **The paradox:** Growing old impairs sight, hearing, cognition, taste, digestion, energy, mobility, and raises vulnerability to heart disease, stroke, atherosclerosis, pneumonia, arthritis, and diabetes. Yet **the elderly feel happier than they did when younger, stronger, and healthier.**
    *   **Carstensen's answer:** Seniors have decided, literally, that they **don't have time** for negativity — the "time" being the rest of their lives. They've mastered the geography of self-influence, going more frequently and fully to the internal and external locations populated by mood-lifting material: they recall positive memories, entertain pleasant thoughts, seek and retain favorable information, search for and gaze at happy faces, and focus on the upsides of their consumer products. **The seniors with the best "attention management" skills — good at orienting to and staying fixed on positive material — show the greatest mood enhancement.** Those with poor attentional control experience mood degeneration with age, and likely account for the irascible-elderly stereotype: "the grumpy are just more conspicuous than the contented." (They don't deny the bad — they accept it and decline to **dwell** on it; in marital conflict the distinctive senior move is to turn focus away from the clash toward pleasanter topics.)
    *   **Why youth can't just copy this:** Younger people's primary life goals are **learning, developing, and striving for achievement** — which requires a special **openness to discomforting elements**: demanding tasks, contrary points of view, unfamiliar people, and owning mistakes or failures. Any other approach would be maladaptive. The problem is only when we become **mired** in the emotions those generate and locked into a cycling loop of negativity.
    *   **Lyubomirsky's activities (the pre-suasive attention-refocusing list):** Asked not "what factors are associated with happiness" but *"which specific activities can we perform to increase our happiness?"* Top three require nothing more than strategic refocusing: (1) **Count your blessings and gratitudes at the start of every day, and write them down**; (2) **Cultivate optimism by choosing beforehand to look on the bright side** of situations, events, and future possibilities; (3) **Negate the negative** by deliberately limiting time spent dwelling on problems or on unhealthy comparisons with others. The bad news: *"You can make yourself happier just like you can make yourself lose weight... you have to put in the effort every day. You have to stay with it."* The elderly don't visit their hospitable inner geographies like sightseers — **they relocate there psychologically, for the warming climate they meet every morning.**
*   **Alan Chaikin — the Pre-Suasive Test-Taker (transferable tactic):** A grad-school classmate scored in the **top 1%** on all three GRE sections plus psychology knowledge. He wasn't generally smarter — he was better at **managing his state**. Two differentiators:
    *   **Tactical sequencing:** He was a speed reader, and since the GRE was scored on raw number correct, he made a first pass answering only the easy/known items, banking every cheap point, then went back for the hard ones. (Most students bog down in difficult items, incurring the twin penalties of wrong answers and never reaching the easy questions.) Test redesigns have since removed this edge.
    *   **Pre-suasive psych-up (the durable technique):** Immediately before any standardized exam — against Cialdini's own habit of cramming shaky facts in the final minutes — Chaikin spent that crucial time **consciously calming his fears and simultaneously building confidence by reviewing past academic successes and enumerating his genuine strengths.** His reasoning: *"You can't think straight when you're scared... plus, you're much more persistent when you're confident in your abilities."* Supporting research: particularly among people who don't usually test well (e.g., low-income individuals), initially describing a personal experience that made them feel proud and successful led to significantly better performance on intelligence test items.
*   **What's Already in Them (Moving Others Toward Outcomes) — the STEM Stereotype-Threat Playbook:**
    *   **The setup:** A superintendent needs documented gains in female students' standardized math scores to win a federal grant. The standard mass-testing protocol — (1) assign rooms by last-name initial, (2) randomly assign teacher monitors, (3) a ten-minute period for students to collect their thoughts and anticipate how they'll handle difficult material, (4) record name/ID/**gender** at the outset — would undermine the goal. Why? A widely held societal stereotype says women aren't as good at math, and **almost anything that causes women to focus up front on that belief reduces their math performance**: raising anxiety (interfering with recall), diverting attention from the test (missing vital information), and attributing difficulty to personal deficit rather than task complexity (giving up too soon). All four standard procedures intensify the stereotype focus. **Four research-based fixes:**
        1.  **Assign test takers to rooms by a relevant factor (gender), not an irrelevant one (last-name initial).** Girls taking math in the same room as boys score worse; in a room with only other women, the drop disappears. Notably, no such drop occurs on **verbal** tests, because there's no societal stereotype that women's verbal capacity is inferior. (Solo-status effects and the mere reminder of gender are the culprits.)
        2.  **Assign monitors tactically, not randomly — girls' monitors should be female science and mathematics teachers.** Evidence that other women have defied the stereotype deflates it. Exposing women students to instances of successful women in science- and math-related fields — including the women administering the test — resulted in significantly more problems solved, **even the most difficult ones.**
        3.  **Eliminate the "collect your thoughts about the hard material" period and replace it with a self-affirmation.** Ask the girls to pick a personal value important to them (maintaining friendships, helping others) and **write down why they find it important**. This directs initial attention to an interpersonal strength and reduces threatening-stereotype effects. In one university physics class, women who did such a self-affirmation exercise just **twice** — once at the outset and once mid-semester — scored a **full letter grade** better on the course's math-intensive exams.
        4.  **Do not ask students to record their gender at the start of a math exam.** Instead ask for **year in school** — which in a senior class is always "graduating senior." That supplants a pre-suasive focus on a perceived academic **shortcoming** with a pre-suasive focus on a perceived academic **accomplishment**. When tried, it eliminated women test takers' math performance deficits.
    *   **The Elegant Proof (dual identity):** Prior to a math test, Asian American women were asked to record either their **gender** or their **ethnicity**. Compared with a control group asked to record neither: those reminded of their **gender scored worse**; those reminded of their **ethnicity scored better** ("Asians do well at math"). **Same person, same questions, same skill — the only difference was which existing identity was elevated in attention at the moment of decision.** (This is the purest demonstration in the book of the pre-suasive mechanism operating inside a person.)
    *   **The Generalization:** The same processes apply beyond gender: Princeton student-athletes reminded of their athlete identity (via the belief that athletes aren't very intelligent) **declined** on a math test; reminding African American students of their race before an exam degrades test performance. And the same buffers work for them: self-affirmations and exposure to successful role models.
    *   **The Hard Truth Cialdini Insists On:** There is little or no objective basis for the belief that women are, on average, less able on math-related tasks — **except when focused on gender**, they normally score as well as men. The underrepresentation of women in STEM careers is largely a matter of **preference**: women are more interested in the operation of **social systems** (communal goals involving interacting with others) than in inorganic numerical, mechanical, and physical systems (interacting with things) — attention differences that appear even in infancy, where girls look at human faces longer than boys do.
## CHAPTER 9: THE MECHANICS OF PRE-SUASION — CAUSES, CONSTRAINTS, AND CORRECTIVES
*   **Readied and Waiting:** *"The readiness is all."* The mechanism rests on an underappreciated property of mental activity: **its elements don't just fire when ready; they fire when readied.** After we attend to an opener concept, closely linked concepts enjoy a **privileged moment in the mind** for two linked reasons:
    1.  **Accessibility:** The associated secondary concepts become more accessible in consciousness, which greatly raises the chance we will attend and respond to the linked concept — and this newly elevated standing lets it color our perceptions, orient our thinking, affect our motivations, and change our behavior.
    2.  **Suppression:** Concepts **not** linked to the opener are suppressed — decommissioned temporarily, less likely than before to receive attention and gain influence.
*   **The Video-Game Proof (and Its Mirror Image):**
    *   **Violent games** incite immediate antisocial behavior — players become more likely to deliver loud blasts of noise into the ears of someone who annoyed them — because the games plant aggression-related thoughts that are then easily accessible.
    *   **Prosocial games** (protecting, rescuing, assisting characters) produce the mirror image: after playing, participants became more willing to **help clean up a spill, volunteer their time, and even intervene in a harassment situation involving a young woman and her ex-boyfriend** — the direct result of easy access to prosocial thoughts.
    *   **The twist that confirms the accessibility account:** Sometimes violent game play **decreases** later aggression — if participants had to **cooperate with one another** in the game to destroy an enemy. Playing cooperatively, even with violent content, **suppresses aggressive thoughts**. The content matters less than which associational network the activity readies.
*   **Question 1 — How Soon? (the primitiveness of the mechanism):** Subjects shown photographs including **two individuals standing close together** (vs. standing apart, or one person alone) were **three times more likely to help** the researcher pick up items she "accidentally" dropped — because togetherness and helpfulness are linked in people's minds. What stuns Cialdini: the subjects were **eighteen months old.** Barely able to talk, unable to reason or reflect — "Yet the mechanism involved is so fundamental to human functioning that even these infants were powerfully mobilized by it." And the effect was **spontaneous** — no prodding or request required. (Adult preview: giving people cues of togetherness increased their enjoyment of working simultaneously on a task, leading to greater persistence and better performance. When unity is made focal, all kinds of desirable concepts besides helpfulness are readied.)
*   **Question 2 — How Far? (the strength of pre-suasive links):**
    *   **The Boundary Condition:** No, not any link, however tenuous, triggers a privileged moment. **Attention to the first concept readies the second in proportion to the degree of association between the two.**
    *   **The Littering Field Study (Cialdini, Reno & Kallgren):** A preliminary survey rated three social norms by distance from the anti-littering norm: **recycling** (close), **turning off lights to conserve energy** (moderately far), and **voting** (far). Researchers placed a handbill on the windshield of each car in a public library parking lot. At random, the handbill carried one of four norm messages — (1) against littering, (2) for recycling, (3) for turning off lights, (4) for voting — plus a control message promoting the local arts museum with no norm at all. Then they watched whether drivers dropped the handbill on the ground.
    *   **The Result Pattern:** The anti-littering message **best** equipped people to resist littering. Directing attention to opener concepts **progressively more distant** from the anti-littering norm made them **less able, at each remove, to resist the impulse.** Closer association → cleaner parking lot.
    *   **The Operational Rule:** *"An aspiring pre-suader wishing to prompt an action (helping, let's say) should find a concept already associated strongly and positively with the action (togetherness would be a good choice) and bring that concept to mind in potential helpers just before requesting their aid."*
*   **Question 3 — How Manufacturable? (creating a connection from scratch):** You need not find an existing strong link. Advertisers have built links out of nothing for over a century: present something that attracts the target audience — a beautiful vista, a good-looking model, a popular celebrity — then link it to the product by **nothing more than simultaneous presence inside the ad.** (Tiger Woods and Buick; Beyoncé and Pepsi; Brad Pitt and Chanel No. 5; Bob Dylan and Victoria's Secret.) The hope: attraction transfers to the product by virtue of the now-extant connection. Almost everybody knows this is happening — **and it works anyway.**
    *   **Pavlov's precedent:** There was no natural connection between a bell and food — none of any sort until the two were experienced as occurring together. After enough pairings, the bell spontaneously readied the animal for eating.
    *   **The Evidence We Are Just as Susceptible and Just as Clueless:**
        *   Superimposing a Belgian beer brand **five times** on pictures of pleasant activities (sailing, waterskiing, cuddling) increased viewers' positive feelings toward the beer.
        *   Superimposing a mouthwash brand **six times** on pictures of beautiful nature scenes made observers more favorable right away — **and still more favorable three weeks afterward.**
        *   Subliminally exposing **thirsty** people **eight times** to pictures of happy (vs. angry) faces just before tasting a new soft drink made them **consume more of the beverage** and be willing to **pay three times more** for it in the store.
        *   In none of these studies were participants aware they had been influenced by the pairings.
    *   **Newer evidence:** Such shifts in attractiveness can occur **without conscious control over them or awareness of them** (contingency-unaware evaluative conditioning).
*   **If/When-Then Plans (the personal application):**
    *   **The Problem:** Generating an intention is not enough. In health, people translate good intentions into action only about **half the time**. Two traced failings: (1) we sometimes forget the intention, and we frequently **don't recognize opportune moments or circumstances** for healthy behavior (e.g., taking stairs instead of the elevator); (2) we are **derailed by distracting factors** — a busy day.
    *   **The Solution:** A category of strategic self-statements — **if/when-then plans** — that ready us (1) to **register certain cues** in settings where we can further the goal, and (2) to take an **appropriate action** spurred by those cues and consistent with the goal.
    *   **Examples:** Weight loss — *"If/when, after my business lunches, the server asks if I'd like to have dessert, then I will order mint tea."* Epilepsy medication — *"When it is eight in the morning, and I finish brushing my teeth, then I will take my prescribed pill dose."* **Adherence rose from 55% to 79%.**
    *   **The Opiate-Addict Demonstration:** Hospitalized opiate addicts in withdrawal were urged to prepare an employment history by day's end to help them get a job after release. Some formed an if/when-then plan; controls did not. Example plan: *"If/when lunch is over and space has become available at the lunchroom table, then I will start writing my employment history there."* **By day's end, not one control subject had performed the task. Among the if/when-then group, 80% had turned in a completed job résumé** — drug addicts in withdrawal.
    *   **Why It Beats Ordinary Intention and Action Plans:** "I intend to lose five pounds this month" or "I plan to lose weight by cutting down on sweets" is **considerably less likely to succeed.** The specific sequencing of if/when-then plans defeats the traditional enemies of goal achievement: the wording puts us on **high alert for a particular time or circumstance** when a productive action could be performed, then **associates it automatically and directly with the desired conduct.** It is also **self-tailored** — we choose the cues and we build the cue-to-action link ourselves.
    *   **Prefetched Sources (the computer analogy):** Most programs have links that need two clicks (ready, then launch) and links that launch with one because they are already hyperlinked — "prefetched." Parents, teachers, leaders, and eventually we ourselves install prefetched sources in our mental programming: attitudes, role identities, cultural frames, goals, and personality orientations that sit on **continuing standby** in consciousness so a single reminder cue (click) launches them. **If/when-then plans put the specification of those reminder cues in our own hands.** Chronically unsuccessful dieters eat fewer high-calorie foods and lose more weight after forming plans like *"If/when I see chocolate displayed in the supermarket, then I will think of my diet."* For goals we're highly committed to, refusing the pre-suasive leverage of if/when-then plans is foolish.
    *   **The Qualifier on Prefetched Sources:** They are constantly **in place** but not constantly **in force**. Almost any cue can activate them — a setting, a TV show, an ad. (Gender: men and women behave identically on math tests **except when reminded of their gender**; culture, self-identity, goals, and personality traits behave the same way.) The attention state is the switch.
*   **The Correction Problem (Can we defend against all this?):** We cannot frontload every situation — we enter new physical environments and social interactions constantly, and we receive persuasion appeals whose components we can't anticipate. In those cases, the answer to whether we're leaves in the wind **"depends on whether we've noticed the breeze."** If our preferences can be swayed by whether a slogan rhymes, a name resembles ours, an ad features a beautiful vista, or a ticker symbol is easy to pronounce, we'd want to correct for it — and often, **simply recognizing the influence is enough to block it.**
*   **Mere Reminders (correction mechanism #1):**
    *   **Mood contamination:** After receiving a mood-enhancing free gift of high-quality writing paper, passersby rated their **cars and TVs as working better**. A man who complimented young women and asked for their phone numbers succeeded **22.4% of the time on sunny mornings vs. 13.9% on cloudy ones.** People surveyed by phone reported themselves **20% more satisfied with their lives as a whole** on sunny vs. rainy days.
    *   **But — the one-question fix:** If the interviewer first asked, *"By the way, how's the weather over there?"* the sunny-versus-rainy effect **didn't materialize at all.** Simply focusing participants on the weather for a moment reminded them of its potentially biasing influence and let them correct their thinking accordingly.
    *   **The Same Pattern for Sports:** Local team victories raise favorability toward — and votes for — incumbent officeholders. But if fans are first asked about the game's outcome and thereby reminded that the reason for their good mood is unrelated to the politicians' performance, the enlarged favorability **shrinks to zero.**
    *   **Gregory Ratoff's "nonsense":** *"Let me ask you a question, for your information."* Cialdini defends it as sensible: **posing a question can provide invaluable information to its recipient** — it spurs them to bring to mind a piece of possessed knowledge that isn't high in consciousness but that, once focal, **changes everything.**
    *   **Theoretical frame — the Flexible Correction Model (Wegener & Petty):** Correction is likely when people **recognize** they are susceptible to an unwanted bias AND have both the **motivation** and the **ability** to counter it. The psychological etymology of the trigger is exact: **remind = re-mind.** All that's required to get people to act on a piece of already-held knowledge is to get them to put their minds on it again immediately before the act.
*   **Signs of Stealthy Persuasive Intent (correction mechanism #2):**
    *   **The Product-Placement Paradox:** Practitioners believe the more perceptible the placement, the more effective — the logic being that prominence increases noticing, which increases influence. The *Seinfeld* study measured both the industry-standard metrics and a third, telling measure. Most prominent placements (brand shown on camera **and** mentioned aloud) produced the most **recognition and recall** — as expected. But when audience members indicated **which brands they would be likely to choose when shopping**, they were **least likely to select the products inserted most prominently.** Subtle placements were chosen by **47%**; the most prominent, by only **27%.** Why? The conspicuousness **cued viewers to the advertiser's sly attempt to sway their preferences** and caused a correction against the potential distortion.
    *   **The Nudge-Too-Far Principle:** People recognize advertisers' practices can unduly influence them, but it takes a **reminder of the source of the possible bias** to trigger rebalancing. The reminder is frequently the trick exposed **too far** — the over-obvious version of the same device. (Related: repeated conspicuous placements in clips also caused attitude drops; subtle placements escaped detection as bias sources even when repeated, and even became slightly more favorable with repetition — the same pattern as the subliminal banner ads.)
    *   *Practical caution for the DMA operator:* this applies to your own marketing. A device that announces itself as a device gets discounted. A device that stays below the noticing threshold keeps working.
*   **Gapping the Mind (why deliberation rarely saves us):**
    *   **The Cost Structure:** Deliberative reasoning — weighing caloric, nutritional, and unit-pricing information against packaging, placement, and advertising — requires **more time, energy, and motivation** than the automatic responses it must offset. When any requirement isn't met, we default to decision shortcuts. Shortcuts often produce good outcomes. "But in many other situations, they can send us to places we didn't want to go — at least not if we'd thought about it."
    *   **Why Infomercials Air Late:** The assumption is lower broadcast fees. The deeper reason: **the ads perform better then.** At the tail of a long day, viewers "don't have the mental energy to resist the ads' emotional triggers — likable hosts, enthusiastic studio audiences, dwindling supplies." (Al Eicoff, an infomercial forefather: *"People are less resistant at that hour. If they're tired, their subconscious will accept without their conscious fighting it."*)
    *   **Fatigue and Obedience:** In field tests, **fully rested** combat artillery teams often challenged orders to fire on hospitals or other civilian targets. After **twenty-four to thirty-six sleepless hours**, they often obeyed superiors' directives without question and became more likely to shell anything.
    *   **Fatigue and False Confessions:** The typical interrogation lasts **less than an hour**. Interrogations that generate false confessions average **sixteen hours.**
    *   **The Full List of Deliberation-Killers:** Being rushed, overloaded, preoccupied, indifferent, stressed, distracted, or — Cialdini adds deadpan — "it seems, a conspiracy theorist."
    *   **The Rushed Condition (the camera study):** Twelve features comparing two cameras. Brand A was superior on the **three most important** features (lens quality, mechanism, pictures). Brand B was superior on **eight relatively unimportant** ones (e.g., a shoulder strap was included).
        *   **Two seconds per feature:** only **17%** preferred the higher-quality camera — the majority took the brand with more unimportant advantages.
        *   **Five seconds per feature:** still only **38%** favored the sensible choice.
        *   **Unlimited time:** the pattern **reversed** — **67%** favored the camera with fewer but more significant advantages.
    *   **The Broadcast Parallel (why the media is a pre-suasion machine):** Think about how you must respond to today's rapid-fire delivery. Broadcast media transmit a swift stream of information that can't be easily slowed or reversed. You cannot focus on the real quality of an advertiser's case in a radio or TV spot; you cannot respond mindfully to a clip of a politician's speech. So you are left focusing on **secondary features** — the attractiveness of the spokesperson, the charisma of the candidate.
    *   **The Modern Multiplier:** The sheer volume of information (its complexity befuddling, its relentlessness depleting, its range distracting, its prospects agitating) plus the concentration-disrupting alerts of the devices nearly everyone carries. *"Thus, a communicator who channels attention to a particular concept in order to heighten audience receptivity to a forthcoming message — via the focus-based, automatic, crudely associative mechanisms of pre-suasion — won't have to worry much about the tactic being defeated by deliberation. The cavalry of deep analysis will rarely arrive to reverse the outcome because it will rarely be summoned."*
*   **The Question That Opens Part 3:** On which concepts, then, should an audience's attention be focused for the **broadest** pre-suasive effect? Seven.
## CHAPTER 10: SIX MAIN ROADS TO CHANGE — BROAD BOULEVARDS AS SMART SHORTCUTS
*   **The Recall of Specificity:** Every opener is goal-specific. *Chocolates at a high price → first have them write down a much larger number. French wine → French background music. Untested product → ask if they're adventurous. Popular item → show a scary movie. Warmth toward you → hand them a hot drink. Helpfulness → show people standing close together. Achievement → an image of a runner winning a race. Careful assessment → Rodin's "The Thinker."* A French song won't make them achievement-oriented; asking about adventurousness won't make them choose the popular item. **Successful openers channel attention only to concepts associated favorably with the communicator's particular goal.**
*   **But Is There a Universal Goal?** Yes — **assent**. Any persuasive communicator wants "Yes." So which concepts align especially well with the broad goal of obtaining agreement? The **six** from *Influence*: **reciprocation, liking, social proof, authority, scarcity, consistency.** They are highly effective general generators of acceptance because they typically counsel people correctly about when to say yes.
*   **The Expansion (the book's key upgrade to *Influence*):** Don't just highlight the principle **inside** your message — pre-suade by highlighting it **in the moment before** the message. *"Communicators stand to be more effective by highlighting the idea of authority not just inside their message but inside the moment before their message."* Done well, audiences become sensitized to — and readied for — the coming authoritative evidence, making them more likely to pay attention to it, assign it importance, and be influenced by it.
    *   **The Hard Caveat:** This only works when the evidence is actually strong. Directing attention to weak evidence **backfires**. (Demonstrated with consistency: people who strongly preferred consistency became more consistent after a consistency-priming opener, while people who didn't prefer it became **even less** consistent. The opener amplifies whatever the underlying disposition is.)

### ROAD 1 — RECIPROCATION (people say yes to those they owe)
*   **The Rule:** Those who have given benefits to us are entitled to benefits from us in return. Every human culture teaches it from childhood and assigns punishing names — freeloader, user, taker, parasite — to those who don't give back.
*   **It Starts Early and Runs Deep:** Children respond to the rule before age two. In one candy store, shoppers became **42% more likely to purchase** if they'd received a free piece of chocolate on entry. Costco's free samples produce big lifts in beer, cheese, frozen pizza, and lipstick — "almost all accounted for by the shoppers who accept the free offer."
*   **The Uncomfortable Consequence:** US companies making sizable campaign contributions to lawmakers on tax-policy committees see significant reductions in their tax rates. The legislators deny any quid pro quo. "But the companies know better. And so should we."
*   **You Have to Give First:** The rule requires something that *feels* risky — providing gifts, favors, advantages, or concessions **before** getting anything. It usually beats the traditional exchange (benefit only after the contract is signed, the purchase made, the task done).
    *   Dutch residents who received an advance letter asking for participation in a long survey were much more likely to agree **if the payment accompanied the letter** rather than being promised afterward.
    *   US hotel guests asked to reuse towels were given one of two additional lines: the hotel had **already** made a contribution to an environmental organization in the guests' name, or it **would** make one after they reused. **The before-the-act donation was 47% more effective.**
*   **The Three Optimizing Features of a First Gift (the operational checklist):** What you give first should be **meaningful, unexpected, and customized.**
    *   **Meaningful:** A New Jersey waitress offering one chocolate per diner raised her tips **3.3%**. When she invited them to take **two**, tips rose **14.1%**. *Meaningful is not the same as expensive — the second chocolate cost pennies.*
    *   **Unexpected:** When she offered one chocolate, turned to walk away, then **returned** and offered a second, average tips improved by **21.3%.**
    *   **Customized (worth more than the other two combined):** Fast-food visitors greeted with an equally priced gift. A **key chain** (not food-related) raised their subsequent spending **12%** over no gift at all. A **cup of yogurt** (food-related — matching the reason they walked in, hunger) raised it to **24%**. Economically absurd; psycho-logically perfect. The rule for reciprocation has a muscular version: *people should feel especially obligated to reciprocate a gift designed to meet their particular needs.*
*   **The Doctrine in the Extreme (proof of force):**
    *   **Abu Jandal,** Osama bin Laden's former chief bodyguard, captured after 9/11 and interrogated in a Yemeni prison: responses were only screeds against the West. Interrogators noticed he never ate the cookies served with his meals — and learned he was **diabetic**. Next session: **sugar-free cookies** with tea. A meaningful, unexpected, customized favor. Per an interrogator: *"We had shown him respect, and we had done this nice thing for him. So he started talking to us instead of giving us lectures."* He provided extensive data on Al Qaeda operations and the names of **seven of the 9/11 hijackers.**
    *   **The CIA's Afghan Tribal Chief:** A chief exhausted by the duties of leading his tribe and immediate family — which included **four younger wives**. The operative returned with a fully optimized gift: **four Viagra tablets, one per wife.** The next visit produced a wealth of information on Taliban movements and supply routes.
    *   **Lord Arthur Weidenfeld:** Arrived in England in 1938 on a Kindertransport — a rescue organized by Christian humanitarian societies that saved thousands of Jewish children. He became a major UK publisher and an English lord. In 2015, at **ninety-four**, he organized and funded **Operation Safe Havens**, transporting threatened Syrian and Iraqi Christian families out of ISIS-held territory. Criticized for not including other equally threatened religious groups, he explained: *"I can't save the world, but... on the Jewish and Christian side... I had a debt to repay."*
*   **The Gratitude Nuance:** In the reciprocity family, **obligation** has an equally active but sweeter sister — **gratitude**. Gratitude stimulates returns not from a sense of debt but from a sense of appreciation, and it **intensifies relationships** rather than just instigating or maintaining them.

### ROAD 2 — LIKING (and the correction that matters most)
*   **The Old Rule and the Rethink:** Sales training asserts: *the number one rule is to get your customer to like you.* Cialdini rethinks it. Similarities and compliments cause people to feel that **you like them** — and once they recognize that you like them, they'll want to do business with you. **People trust that those who like them will try to steer them correctly.** So: *"The number one rule for salespeople is to show customers that you genuinely like them."* The maxim: **"People don't care how much you know until they know how much you care."**
*   **Similarities (the mechanisms and the payoff):**
    *   Infants smile more at adults whose facial expressions match their own.
    *   **Language-style matching** (shared words and verbal expressions) increases romantic attraction, relationship stability, and — remarkably — the likelihood that a **hostage negotiation** ends peacefully. The overlap typically goes **unnoticed** by both partners.
    *   People are massively more willing to help an emergency victim if they share a **nationality** or even a **favorite sports team**.
    *   In youth mentoring programs, the **largest** determinant of success is the initial **similarity of interests** between student and mentor.
    *   **The commercial effects:** waitresses coached to mimic customers' verbal style **doubled their tips**. Negotiators coached to mimic opponents' style got significantly better final outcomes. Salespeople who mimicked customers' language styles and nonverbal behaviors (gestures, postures) **sold more** of the electronics they recommended.
*   **Compliments (flattery is a fuel):** Mark Twain: *"I can live for two months on a good compliment."* Praise works whether for appearance, taste, personality, work habits, or intelligence. Hair-salon stylists who said *"Any hairstyle would look good on you"* raised their tips by **37%**. Chinese college students who got a preprinted flier reading *"We're contacting you because you're fashionable and stylish"* developed more positive attitudes toward the store and were more likely to want to shop there. Participants who received flattering **task-related feedback from a computer** — after being told the feedback was preprogrammed and reflected nothing about their actual performance — became **prouder of their performances** anyway. "Flattery... will get you everywhere." (Mae West)

### ROAD 3 — SOCIAL PROOF (the crowd as a shortcut to right and to possible)
*   **The Principle:** People think it is appropriate to believe, feel, or do something to the extent that others — especially **comparable** others — are believing, feeling, or doing it. The perceived appropriateness runs on two components: **validity** (it's right) and **feasibility** (it's doable).
*   **Validity (the moral and practical "right"):**
    *   Learning that the majority of their peers supported the military's use of torture made **80%** of group members find the practice **more acceptable** — and demonstrate greater support in public statements and, more revealingly, in **private opinions**.
    *   The mirror-image good news: professionals told that the great majority of people **try to overcome their stereotypes** became more resistant to stereotypes in their own work-related conduct.
    *   **Beijing restaurants:** Labeling dishes as **"most popular"** made each dish **13% to 20% more popular** — at zero cost (no new ingredients, staff, or flowery menu descriptions).
    *   **Dutch high schools:** Rather than assembling and communicating extensive nutritional information, simply telling students that the **majority of their schoolmates do try to eat fruit to be healthy** increased fruit consumption by **35%** — even though the students "claimed no intention to change."
    *   **Pollution abatement:** Nations that rate polluting firms within an industry and then **publicize the ratings** so every company can see where it stands relative to peers produced overall improvements **upward of 30%** — almost all of it coming from the **heaviest polluters**, who saw how poorly they compared.
    *   **Online auctions:** This is why lower starting prices generate **higher final purchase prices** — low starting prices attract more bidders, and later bidders see all the interest and infer inherent worth rather than an enticing opening price.
*   **Feasibility (the more underrated half):**
    *   **The Energy Conservation Field Study:** Four messages delivered weekly for a month asking households to reduce energy use — (1) it benefits the environment, (2) it's the socially responsible thing to do, (3) it will save you significant money on your next power bill, (4) most of your fellow community residents **do try** to conserve energy at home. The **social-proof message generated 3.5x as much energy savings** as any other message. The homeowners themselves predicted the social-proof message would be the **least** effective.
    *   **The Utility Executive Objection and the Answer:** "How are we supposed to believe that telling people their neighbors are conserving is three times more effective than telling them they can cut their power bills significantly?" The answer is feasibility: *"I could reduce my next power bill to zero if I turned off all the electricity in my house and curled up on the floor in the dark for a month; but that's not something I'd reasonably do."* Social proof **destroys the problem of uncertain achievability**: if many others like them are doing it, its feasibility is established. It becomes realistic, and therefore implementable.
    *   **The Generalization:** Social comparison drives health behavior because it determines how **manageable** an action seems to a given individual.

### ROAD 4 — AUTHORITY (the messenger is the message)
*   **Three Claims About What Carries the Message:** "The merit is the message" (get the content right). "The medium is the message" (McLuhan — the channel shapes how recipients experience content). And **"The messenger is the message."**
*   **The Brain-Scan Evidence:** Participants making difficult economic decisions while hooked to brain-scanning equipment. On their own, activity jumped in the areas associated with **evaluating options**. When they received expert advice (from a distinguished university economist), they **followed it without thinking about the inherent merits of the options** — related activity in the evaluating sectors **flatlined**. Tellingly, the sectors associated with understanding another's intentions **were** activated. The messenger had become the focal message.
*   **Authority vs. In Authority:** The relevant figure is not necessarily someone with hierarchical power who can **command** assent — it's someone who **is** an authority and can **induce** assent through recognized **expertise**.
*   **The Credible Authority = Expertise + Trustworthiness.** We've covered expertise. Now trustworthiness — and here is the tactical gold:
*   **Instant Trustworthiness (the weakness-first sequence):**
    *   **The Standard Mistake:** Present all the most favorable features up front and reserve any drawbacks for the end — or never.
    *   **The Correct Sequence:** Reference a **weakness early**. Perceived truthfulness is then established pre-suasively, so when the major strengths are advanced, **the audience is more likely to believe them** — they've been conveyed by a source whose honesty is already established by a willingness to point at negatives.
    *   **Documented In:** (1) **Legal settings** — a trial attorney who admits to a weakness before the rival attorney points it out is viewed as more credible and **wins more often**; (2) **Political campaigns** — a candidate who begins with something positive to say about an opponent gains trustworthiness **and voting intentions**; (3) **Advertising** — merchandisers who acknowledge a drawback before highlighting strengths often see large increases in sales.
    *   **Use the Transitional Word:** *however, but, yet* — which channels listeners' attention **away from the weakness and onto a countervailing strength.** "I am not experienced in this field, **but** I am a very fast learner." "Our set-up costs are not the lowest; **however**, you'll recoup them quickly due to our superior efficiencies."
    *   **The Two Optimal Conditions:** (a) The tactic is **particularly strong when the audience already knows the weakness** — mentioning it adds no new damage, only the information that the communicator is honest. (b) The strength that follows should **challenge the relevance of the weakness** — not simply add unrelated positivity.
    *   **Queen Elizabeth I (both conditions, both speeches):** At Tilbury in 1588, addressing troops massed against an expected Spanish invasion, she dispelled concern that a woman couldn't lead in battle: *"I know I have the body of a weak and feeble woman; **but** I have the heart of a king, and a king of England, too!"* The cheering was so long and loud officers had to ride among the men ordering restraint so she could continue. The weakness (a woman's body) was **undercut by the strength** (a king's heart) — not balanced out. Thirteen years later, in her final formal remarks to a Parliament that largely mistrusted her: *"And though you have had, and may have, many mightier and wiser princes sitting in this seat, **yet** you have never had, nor shall have, any that will love you better."* Audience members left "transfigured, many of them in tears," and named it the **"Golden Speech"** that same day.
    *   **Named Campaigns:** Avis — *"We're #2. We try harder."* Volkswagen Beetle — *"Ugly is only skin deep"* and *"It's ugly but it gets you there."* Buckley's cough mixture — *"It Tastes Awful. And It Works."* Domino's Pizza's brutally honest 2009 campaign admitting past poor quality. In each case, sales went up. **The pre-suasive leverage of honesty-about-a-flaw is one of the most reliable mechanisms in this book.**
    *   **The Authority Label (an unused freebie):** The receptionist in a realty firm was already connecting new callers to the relevant expert agent. Adding the honest sentence *"I'll connect you with our **expert agent** in your area of interest"* raised the number of callers who became customers by **16%**. She hadn't changed anything but the label. Similarly, anti-smoking programs for children become significantly more effective when a **doctor** recommends them — which doctors frequently fail to do.

### ROAD 5 — SCARCITY (we want more of what we can have less of)
*   **The Original Demonstrations:** When the pastry chain Crumbs announced it would close all locations in 2014, its $4 signature cupcakes commanded up to **$250 apiece online**. Outside an Apple Store, a woman waiting for a new iPhone traded her **$2,800 Louis Vuitton shoulder bag** for a stranger's earlier place in line — because "I heard that this store didn't have a big supply, and I didn't want to risk losing the chance to get one."
*   **Loss Aversion Is the Engine:** Loss is the ultimate form of scarcity — it renders the valued item or opportunity **unavailable**. A brokerage CEO's mentor: *"If you wake a multimillionaire client at five in the morning and say, 'If you act now, you will gain twenty thousand dollars,' he'll scream at you and slam down the phone. But if you say, 'If you don't act now, you will lose twenty thousand dollars,' he'll thank you."*
*   **Scarcity Raises Judged Value, Not Just the Threat of Loss:** When auto manufacturers limit production of a new model, its value rises among buyers. In one large grocery chain, promotions that included a purchase limit — **"Only x per customer"** — **more than doubled sales** for seven different types of products compared with promotions for the same products with no limit. Follow-up studies showed why: **in the consumer's mind, any constraint on access increased the worth of what was being offered.**
*   **The Other Reasons Scarcity Bites:** People automatically believe rare items have higher economic value. And they **dislike having their freedom to possess an item restricted** — so they choose the scarce item to **restore that freedom** (psychological reactance).
*   **Pre-Suasive Application:** The scarce item is the focal item; and (per Ch. 3–4) whatever is focal is judged both more important and more causal of the outcome. Scarcity, urgency, and exclusivity pre-suade *before* the offer is even read.

### ROAD 6 — CONSISTENCY (a pre-suasive small step unlocks a large one)
*   **The Rule:** We want to be — and to be seen — consistent with our existing commitments: our previous statements, stands we've taken, and actions we've performed. So a communicator who gets us to take a **small pre-suasive step** in the direction of an idea or entity increases our willingness to take a much **larger, congruent** step when asked.
*   **The Prayer Study (an unusual but rigorous case):** Psychologists located a pre-suasive activity that reduces sexual infidelity: prayer — not prayer in general, but a **particular kind**. If one romantic partner agrees to **pray daily for the other's well-being** over an extended period, he or she becomes less likely to be unfaithful while doing so. (Merely thinking positive thoughts about a partner daily, or praying in an unspecified way, did **not** have the same effect.) Such behavior would be inconsistent with an actively made daily commitment to the partner's welfare.
*   **Commercial Applications:**
    *   **Honesty pledges:** Automobile insurance companies reduce policyholders' misreporting of odometer readings by putting the honesty pledge at the **beginning** of the reporting form rather than at the end (signing at the top — a small commitment — pre-suades honest reporting below).
    *   **Voting:** Political parties increase the chance supporters vote in the next election by having arranged for them to vote in the **previous** one.
    *   **Brands:** Customer loyalty deepens when customers are first induced to **recommend** the brand to a friend.
    *   **Attendance (the tiny wording change):** Switching a reminder call's close from *"We'll mark you on the list as coming then. Thank you!"* to *"We'll mark you on the list as coming then, **okay?** [pause for confirmation] Thank you"* — a spoken micro-commitment — raised participation in a blood drive from **70% to 82.4%**.
*   **Sometimes You Need Only Remind Them:** Practitioners can leverage consistency **without installing a new commitment** — simply by reminding people of an existing commitment that fits the goal. (Asking online survey participants to reflect back on **past helping actions** made them **3.5x more likely** to contribute to a fund for new earthquake victims.)
*   **The Marriage Equality Campaign (a masterclass in reminder-based consistency):**
    *   The legal team arguing before the US Supreme Court in 2013 structured a **months-long national PR campaign around one man: Justice Anthony Kennedy.** Two reasons: he was widely considered the deciding vote in both companion cases, and he was a **frequent fence-sitter** — a traditionalist who believed law shouldn't drift far from its original language, **and** a believer that law is a living thing with evolving meanings.
    *   Because he held a foot in each camp, the goal was **not** to change one of his views but to **connect only one of them** to marriage equality.
    *   **The Mechanism:** The campaign saturated the media with a set of concepts and wordings **Kennedy himself had used in prior court opinions**: **"human dignity," "individual liberty," and "personal freedoms/rights."** Wherever he went before oral arguments, he encountered those issues linked to that selected set of his own stated views. The intent: get him to perceive his **prior pertinent legal stances** as associated with the pro-equality position.
    *   Once hearings began, legal team members repeatedly built their **in-court arguments** from the same Kennedy-tied language and themes.
    *   **The Outcome:** The court ruled **5-to-4** in favor of marriage equality. Members of the legal team credit the tactic, pointing to the fact that in his written opinions Kennedy leaned heavily on **dignity, liberty, and freedoms/rights** — the three concepts they had labored to prioritize within his marriage-equality-related thinking before and during the hearings. And two years later, in another marriage-equality case, those same three concepts figured prominently once again in a Justice Kennedy majority opinion. *Pre-suasion, properly executed, can reach across years.*
*   **THE CORE MOTIVES MODEL (Gregory Neidert) — which principle to deploy at which stage:**
    *   **Stage 1 — Cultivate a Positive Association.** People are more favorable to a communication if they are favorable to the communicator. **Use Reciprocity + Liking.** Give first (meaningfully, unexpectedly, customized), highlight genuine commonalities, offer true compliments. This establishes rapport that facilitates all future dealings.
    *   **Stage 2 — Reduce Uncertainty.** A positive relationship doesn't ensure persuasive success. Before people change, they want to see the decision as **wise**. **Use Social Proof + Authority.** Evidence that a choice is well-regarded by peers or experts significantly increases confidence in its wisdom.
    *   **Stage 3 — Motivate Action.** A well-liked friend can show me proof that experts recommend — and nearly all my peers believe — that daily exercise is good, and I still won't do it. **Use Consistency + Scarcity** — reminding me of what I've said publicly in the past about the importance of my health, and the unique enjoyments I'd miss if I lost it. "That's the message that would most likely get me up in the morning and off to the gym."
*   **The Seventh Principle:** For years, asked whether he'd identified any new principles, Cialdini answered no. He now believes there is a **seventh universal principle** — one he missed not because of some new cultural or technological shift, but **because it was hiding beneath the surface of his data all along.**
## CHAPTER 11: UNITY 1 — BEING TOGETHER (THE SEVENTH PRINCIPLE)
*   **The Christmas Card Story (how the principle was found):** For years Cialdini lectured on a study showing that sending holiday cards to total strangers produced a dutiful return card — attributing it to **reciprocity**. After one lecture, an older student told him it had solved a decade-long mystery in her home: ten years earlier her family had received a Christmas card from the Harrisons of Santa Barbara; they knew no Harrisons, assumed a mis-addressed envelope, and **sent one back out of obligation.** Then her youngest son, Skip, was starting at UC Santa Barbara but his dorm room wasn't ready for a few days; she didn't like the motel option, thought *"Who do we know in Santa Barbara? The Harrisons!"* — called, and the strangers happily took him in.
*   **Why Reciprocity Couldn't Explain It:** The obligation was fully discharged. Cards (and year-end letters) had been exchanged **equally**; the two families were even. So the rule for reciprocation may have started the process, but it was the **ten-year resultant relationship** that compelled the Harrisons to open their home to an eighteen-year-old they'd never met. *"That realization made me appreciate the freestanding power of social connections to generate assent — separate from the other six principles of influence. Relationships not only intensify willingness to help but also cause it."*
*   **The Warning:** *"It's a poor trade-off, then, for social influence when we allow present-day forces of separation — distancing societal changes, insulating modern technologies — to take a shared sense of human connection out of our exchanges. The relation gets removed, leaving just the ships, passing at sea."*
*   **THE DEFINITION — the distinction that matters:** Unity is *not* "that person is like us." It is **"that person is OF us."** Cialdini may share far more tastes and preferences with a work colleague than with a sibling, but there is no question which one he would consider *of* him and which merely *like* him — and which he would more likely help in a time of need. **The experience of unity is not about simple similarities** (those work too, but to a lesser degree, via the liking principle). **It's about shared identities** — the categories individuals use to define themselves and their groups: race, ethnicity, nationality, family, political and religious affiliation. The defining characteristic: **members tend to feel at one with, merged with, the others — the conduct of one member influences the self-esteem of other members.** Put simply: **we is the shared me.**
*   **The Evidence of Overlapping Selves:**
    *   People **fail to distinguish correctly between themselves and in-group members**: unduly assigning their own characteristics to those others; repeatedly failing to recall which personal traits they had rated previously for in-group members versus for themselves; and taking **significantly longer** to identify traits that differentiated in-group members from themselves — all reflecting a confusion of self and other.
    *   **Neuroscience explanation:** mental representations of the concepts of self and of close others emerge from the **same brain circuitry**. Activating either concept can lead to neuronal **cross-excitation** of the other, and consequent blurring of identities. (Same structures underpin other confusions — performing an action and imagining performing it share brain components; social rejection hurts in the same regions as physical pain, which is why Tylenol reduces both.)
    *   **The Inclusion of Other in the Self Scale (Aron, 1992):** pairs of overlapping circles; respondents circle the picture that best describes their relationship with a partner. The standard instrument for measuring self-other overlap and for identifying what produces it.
*   **The Two Routes to We-ness:** (1) particular ways of **being** together — this chapter; and (2) particular ways of **acting** together — next chapter.

### BEING TOGETHER — ROUTE 1: KINSHIP
*   **The Biology:** From a genetic point of view, being in the same bloodline is the ultimate form of self–other unity. **Inclusive fitness** undermines the distinction between self and related others: individuals do not so much attempt to ensure their own survival as the survival of **copies of their genes**. The crucial implication: **the self in self-interest can lie outside one's own body and inside the skin of another who shares a goodly amount of genetic material.**
*   **The Behavioral Proof:** People are particularly willing to help genetically close relatives — especially in survival-related decisions. Kidney donation in the United States; rescuing someone from a burning building in Japan; intervening in an axe fight in the jungles of Venezuela. Brain imaging identifies a proximate cause: **people experience unusually high stimulation in the self-reward centers of their brains after aiding a family member — it's almost as if by doing so they are aiding themselves.** This holds **even for teenagers.**
*   **The Best Influence Technique of Cialdini's Career:** He wanted to compare college students' attitudes with their parents' on an array of topics — meaning both groups had to fill out the same lengthy questionnaire. Students were easy: he assigned it as a course exercise in a large psychology class. Parents were the problem: no money to offer, and adult survey participation rates are often **below 20%**. A colleague suggested playing the kinship card — **offering one extra point on the next test to each student whose parent responded.** The effect was astounding: all **163** students sent the questionnaire to a parent, and **159 of them (97%) mailed back a completed copy within a week.** For one point, on one test, in one course, in one semester, for one of their children. Cialdini believes a grandparent version would have returned 162 of 163 — "the missing copy would probably be due to a grandfather's hospitalization from cardiac arrest while he was sprinting to the post office."
*   **Fictive Families (how non-kin hijack kinship):** Collectives that create we-ness among members are characterized by the use of **familial images and labels** — *brothers, sisterhood, forefathers, motherland, heritage* — which lead to increased willingness to sacrifice one's own interests for the group's welfare. Because humans are symbolizing creatures, these imagined "fictive families" produce levels of self-sacrifice typically associated with **highly interrelated clans.** In one pair of studies, reminding Spaniards of the family-like nature of their national ties led those feeling **"fused"** with their fellow citizens to become immediately and dramatically more willing to **fight and die for Spain.**
*   **The Lone Communicator Harnessing Kinship — Warren Buffett's Fiftieth-Anniversary Letter (Feb 2015):**
    *   **The context:** Berkshire Hathaway's financial attainments created a communication problem — how to give current and prospective shareholders confidence that the success will continue. Without it, holders might sell and buyers might go elsewhere. Implicit in the fiftieth anniversary was a reasserting concern in online commentary: Buffett and Munger were clearly no youngsters, and if either departed, prospects and share price could tumble. Cialdini read it and was troubled: was it time to take his quadrupled profits before they evaporated?
    *   **Buffett's standard, and pre-suasive, moves:** *Influence* routinely: he establishes credibility early (first page or two) by **describing a mistake he's made or a problem the company encountered**, examining its implications — rather than burying, minimizing, or papering over difficulties. Then, when he describes the formidable strengths of the company, **readers have already been pre-suaded to trust them more deeply.** He doesn't have a compelling case to *have*; he makes the case compellingly.
    *   **The anniversary letter's ladder:** (1) Reestablish trustworthiness first: *"Now let's look at the road ahead. Bear in mind that if I had attempted 50 years ago to gauge what was coming, certain of my predictions would have been off the mark."* (2) Then something Cialdini had **never seen him do in any public forum**: *"With that warning, I will tell you what I would say to **my family** today if they asked me about Berkshire's future."* (3) Then the careful construction of the case — the proven business model, the bulwark of financial assets, the scrupulously vetted future CEO.
    *   **The result:** As convincing as those components were on their merits, Mr. Buffett had pre-suasively done something that made them **more** convincing: claimed he was going to advise Cialdini about them **as he would a family member**. Because of everything Cialdini knew about the man, he believed the claim — and has never since thought seriously about selling. *"In his letter, Mr. Buffett had me at family."*
    *   **The Meta-Lesson:** He begins that all-important section on page 24 of the report, and he recommends in the report's opening lines that shareholders **jump ahead and read that frame-setting letter before anything else.** Munger's companion anniversary letter used the trustworthiness-enhancing mistake-disclosure too. And — the tell — in the flood of favorable reaction, with headlines like "Warren Buffett just wrote his best annual letter ever," **no one remarked on the familial frame.** *"In the world of hard-minded, fact-based financial investing, the default is to focus on the merit of the message. And, of course, it's true that the merit (of the arguments) can be the message."* But: the **medium** can be the message; the **multitude** can be the message; the **messenger** can be the message; and now — **"the merger (of self and other) can be the message."**
    *   **The Ethics Note:** Cialdini does not view Buffett's or Munger's use of these tactics as trickery. Rather: *"genuinely trustworthy communicators can also be smart enough... to recognize the benefits of gaining pivotal trust through pre-suasive, truthful disclosures."*

### BEING TOGETHER — ROUTE 2: PLACE
*   **The Bridge From Kinship:** No one can look inside another to determine the percentage of genes they share. So to operate in an evolutionarily prudent fashion, people rely on **aspects of another that are simultaneously detectable and associated with genetic overlap** — the most evident being physical and personal similarity.
    *   Inside families, individuals are more helpful to kin they resemble.
    *   Outside the family unit, people use **facial similarity** to judge (fairly accurately) their degree of genetic relatedness to strangers — **and can be tricked into misplaced favoritism.** Observers of a photograph digitally modified to look more like them come to **trust that person to a greater extent**. If the modified face belongs to a political candidate, they become **more willing to vote for him or her**.
    *   *Attitudinal variation:* people also use **attitudinal similarities** to assess genetic relatedness and to form in-groups and decide whom to help. But not all attitudes are equivalent: **fundamental religious and political attitudes** — toward sexual behavior, liberal/conservative ideology — function most forcefully to determine in-group identity, for an instructive reason: these are the attitudes most likely to be **passed on through heredity** and therefore to reflect the genetic "we." Such highly heritable attitudes are also highly resistant to change, perhaps because people are less willing to shift on positions that define them.
*   **HOME:**
    *   Humans and animals react to those present in their homes while growing up **as if they are relatives** — a clue to relatedness that is normally accurate because people in the home typically are family. **The longer the length of coresidence, the greater the effect on individuals' sense of family and their willingness to sacrifice for one another.**
    *   **But there is a related factor producing the same consequences without extensive time together: when people observe their parents caring for the needs of another in the home,** they also experience a family-like feeling and become more willing to give to that other. An intriguing upshot: **children who see their parents open their homes to a range of differing people should be more likely, as adults, to help strangers.** For them, we-ness should reach beyond immediate or extended family **and apply to the human family as well.**
*   **THE SUGIHARA CASE (home as the origin of vast benevolence):**
    *   **The setup:** Near dawn on a summer day in **1940**, two hundred Polish Jews crowded outside the **Japanese consulate in Lithuania** to plead for help escaping the Nazi advance. A puzzle in itself: Nazi Germany and Imperial Japan had close ties and shared interests, and would sign the Tripartite Pact months later. Why would Hitler's targets throw themselves on the mercy of his future ally?
    *   **The historical anomaly:** Before its close strategic associations with Nazi Germany developed in the late 1930s, Japan had allowed displaced Jews easy access to Japanese territories to gain financial resources and political goodwill. Support for the plan remained strong in some Japanese circles, so the government **never completely revoked** its policies of granting travel visas to European Jews. The paradoxical result: in the prewar years, as most countries of the world — **the United States included** — were turning away the desperate prey of Hitler's Final Solution, it was **Japan, Hitler's ally**, that provided them sanctuary in Japanese-controlled **Shanghai** and **Kobe**.
    *   **Chiune Sugihara (an unlikely rescuer):** Midcareer diplomat, consul general in Lithuania by virtue of sixteen years of committed and obedient service. Right credentials for the corps: son of a government official and a samurai family. Set his goals high — proficient in Russian, hoping someday to be Japanese ambassador to Moscow. A great lover of games, music, and parties.
    *   **The decision:** After speaking with the crowd, he wired Tokyo for permission to authorize travel visas. His superiors feared continuing the policies would damage relations with Hitler. **His request was denied, as were his more urgent second and third petitions.** At that point — age forty, no hint of prior disloyalty or disobedience — he began writing the needed travel documents **in outright defiance of his clearly stated, and twice restated, orders.**
    *   **The cost:** Within a month, transferred from his consul general post to a much diminished position outside Lithuania where he could no longer operate independently. Ultimately **expelled from the Foreign Ministry for insubordination.** In dishonor after the war, he sold lightbulbs for a living.
    *   **The persistence:** In the weeks before the consulate closed, he interviewed applicants from early morning to late night. After the consulate was shut and he had moved to a hotel, **he continued writing visas.** After the strain left him thinned and exhausted; after the same strain left **his wife incapable of nursing their infant child**; **on the platform for the train** set to take him from his petitioners; **on the train itself** — he wrote and thrust life-granting papers into life-grasping hands, **saving thousands of innocents.** And when the train began to draw him away, he **bowed deeply and apologized** to those he had to leave stranded — begging their forgiveness for his deficiencies as a helper.
    *   **The home-based factor:** His father, a tax official sent to Korea for a time, moved the family there and **opened an inn.** Sugihara remembered being powerfully affected by his parents' willingness to **take in a broad mix of guests**, tending to their basic needs for food and shelter in the family's home — even providing **baths and washing their clothes** — despite the fact that some were too impoverished to pay. In an interview forty-five years later he said the **nationality and religion of the Jews did not matter**; it mattered only that **they were members with him in the human family, and they needed his help.**
    *   **The Parental Prescription:** *"Give them contact in the home with individuals from a wide spectrum of backgrounds and treat those individuals there like family."* (Corroborating research: Oliner & Oliner found this history in a sizable sample of European Gentiles who harbored Jews — and the rescuers felt a sense of commonality with a more varied group of people than comparable nonrescuers. Half a century later, rescuers were still helping a greater variety of people and causes.)
*   **LOCALITY:**
    *   Humans evolved as a species from small but stable groupings of genetically related individuals, so we favor people who, outside the home, exist in **close proximity.** There is even a named "-ism" for it: **localism.**
    *   **The Concentration Camp Guard (Ronald Cohen's account):** When one prisoner violated a rule, it was not uncommon for all to be lined up and a guard to walk the line counting to ten, **stopping only to shoot each tenth person dead.** A veteran guard performing this task as routinely as always came upon one seemingly unfortunate tenth prisoner, **raised an eyebrow, and executed the eleventh.** Practical explanations are conceivable (past good effort, visible strength or intelligence, future productive work) — but the guard's stated justification, when another guard asked, was simple and sufficient: **he had recognized the man as being from his home town.** Cohen's own comment on the contradiction: *"[W]hile engaged dutifully in mass murder, the guard was merciful and sympathetic to one particular member of the victimized group."* The factor potent enough to turn a cold killer carrying out mass murder into a (specifically focused) merciful enactor was **mutuality of place.**
    *   **The Holocaust Rescuers (the ordinary mechanism):** In the great majority of instances, the rescuers who housed, fed, and hid targets of Nazi persecution **did not spontaneously seek out the targets** to offer help — and, even more notably, **were typically not asked for that help by the victims themselves.** The direct requester was most frequently a **relative or neighbor** petitioning on behalf of a hunted individual or family. In a real sense: *"these rescuers didn't so much say yes to the needy strangers as to their own relatives and neighbors."*
    *   **André Trocmé (the strategic multiplier):** After taking in an initial, lone refugee outside his door — a Jewish woman he found freezing outside his home in December 1940 — he persuaded other residents of his small French town, **Le Chambon-sur-Lignon**, to sustain, harbor, conceal, and smuggle away **thousands** of Jews (an estimated 3,500 lives saved). The instructive feature is not how he arranged care for the first refugee but **how he arranged for the many**: he began by **requesting the help of individuals who would have a difficult time saying no to him — his relatives and neighbors — and then pressed them to do the same among their relatives and neighbors.** His strategic leveraging of existing unities made him not merely a compassionate hero but an **inordinately successful** one. When Vichy officials in custody demanded the names of the Jews he and his neighbors had assisted, his answer could have come from Sugihara: **"We do not know what a Jew is. We only know human beings."**
*   **REGION:**
    *   Even being from the same general geographical region can lead to we-ness. Around the globe, sports team championships stimulate feelings of **personal pride in residents of the team's surrounding zones** — as if the residents had won.
    *   **US Evidence:** citizens agreed to participate in a survey to a greater extent if it emanated from a **home-state university**; readers of a news story about a military fatality in Afghanistan became **more opposed to the war** upon learning the fallen soldier was from their **own state**; and in the Civil War, infantrymen from the **same region** as one another were **less likely to desert.**
    *   **THE RABBI MEETING (the most impressive persuasive communication Cialdini has encountered in thirty-plus years):**
        *   **The situation:** Chiune Sugihara's visas saved thousands, but on arrival in Japanese-held territory they joined an even larger refugee community in Kobe and Shanghai. After the 1941 attack on Pearl Harbor, all refugee passage in and out of Japan ended, and the community's safety became precarious: Japan was by then a full-fledged wartime conspirator with Hitler and had to protect alliance solidarity with a virulent anti-Semite. Worse, in **January 1942** Hitler formalized the annihilation of Jewry at the **Wannsee Conference.** With the Final Solution installed as Axis policy, Nazi officials pressed Tokyo to extend it to Japan's Jews — **proposals involving death camps, medical experiments, and mass drownings at sea** were forwarded to Tokyo after the conference.
        *   **And yet:** despite the potentially damaging impact on its relations with Hitler, **the Japanese government resisted these pressures in early 1942 and maintained that resistance through the end of the war.**
        *   **Why?** The Nazis had sent **Josef Meisinger**, a Gestapo colonel known as the "Butcher of Warsaw" (he had ordered the execution of sixteen thousand Poles), to Tokyo. Arriving in **April 1941**, Meisinger pressed for a policy of brutality toward the Jews under Japan's rule and offered to help design and enact it. Uncertain at first and wanting to hear all sides, high-ranking members of Japan's military government **called upon the Jewish refugee community to send two leaders to a meeting that would significantly influence their future.**
        *   **The two men:** **Rabbi Moses Shatzkes** — renowned as a studious man, one of the most brilliant Talmudic scholars in Europe before the war. **Rabbi Shimon Kalisch** — much older, known for a remarkable ability to understand basic human workings, *"a social psychologist of sorts."*
        *   **The two questions:** Standing before a tribunal of powerful members of the Japanese High Command — who would determine their community's survival — the rabbis faced: **Why do our allies the Nazis hate you so much? And why should we take your side against them?**
        *   **The answer:** Shatzkes, comprehending the tangled complexity of the historical, religious, and economic issues, had no ready response. But Kalisch's knowledge of human nature equipped him to deliver, calmly: **"Because we are Asian, like you."**
        *   **Why it worked (the mechanism in full):** Brief as it was, the assertion was **inspired**. It shifted the Japanese officers' reigning in-group identity **from one based in a temporary wartime alliance to one based in a regional, genetically related mutuality.** It did so by implicating the Nazis' **own racial claim** that the "superior" Aryan master race was innately different from the peoples of Asia. Within a single penetrating observation, **it was the Jews who were aligned with the Japanese and the Nazis who (self-proclaimedly) were not.** The older rabbi's response had a powerful effect: after a silence, the officers conferred among themselves and announced a recess. When they returned, the most senior military official rose and granted the reassurance the rabbis had hoped to bring home: **"Go back to your people. Tell them we will provide for their safety and peace. You have nothing to fear while in Japanese territory."** And so it was.
    *   **The Chapter's Close:** The unitizing powers of family and of place can be harnessed by a skilled communicator — witness the effectiveness of Warren Buffett and Rabbi Kalisch. But there is another kind of unitizing effect available: not from being together in the same genealogy or geography, but from **acting together** synchronously or collaboratively.
## CHAPTER 12: UNITY 2 — ACTING TOGETHER
*   **The Visceral Evidence (Professor Wilhelmina Wosinska's Poland):** Growing up in Soviet-controlled Poland in the 1950s and 1960s meant shortages of basic commodities and dispiriting limits on speech, privacy, information, dissent, and travel. Yet she and her schoolmates were led to register the limits **positively** — as necessary for establishing a fair and equal social order. Those feelings were stoked by celebratory events in which participants **sang and marched together while waving flags in unison.** The effects, she says, were physically stirring, emotionally uplifting, and psychologically validating. She has never felt more impelled by "All for one, and one for all" than in the midst of those scrupulously choreographed involvements. And every time she describes it — even in a sober academic presentation — her voice gains volume, her face gains blood, her eyes gain light. **There is something indelibly visceral about synchronized experience.**
*   **The Anthropological Record:** All human societies have developed ways to respond together — in unison or synchrony — inside songs, marches, rituals, chants, prayers, and dances. They've done so since prehistoric times; **collective dance is depicted extraordinarily often** in the drawings, rock art, and cave paintings of the Neolithic and Chalcolithic periods (e.g., a cave painting at Bhimbetka, India).
*   **Why:** *"When people act in unitary ways, they become unitized."* The resultant feeling of group solidarity serves societies' interests well — producing degrees of loyalty and self-sacrifice usually associated with **much smaller family units.** Human societies, even ancient ones, seem to have discovered group-bonding "technologies" involving coordinated responding, with effects similar to kinship: **we-ness, merger, and the confusion of self and other.**
*   **The Feeling Is Easy to Manufacture:**
    *   **Matching Choice Study:** Participants played a game in which, to win money, they had to make either the **same** choice as their partner or a **different** choice. Those who had to win by **matching** came to see their partners as **more comparable to them** — something about performing in the same way led to greater perceived likeness.
    *   **Sensory Synchrony Study:** Participants watched a video of a stranger's face being stroked with a soft brush, while their own faces were brushed either **identically** (same direction and sequence) or **differently**. Those given the matched sensory experience rated themselves and the depicted stranger as **more alike in both looks and personality.** Even more remarkably, a **blurred sense of self-other identity** emerged: *"It felt as if my face was turning into the face in the video."* *"Sometimes I had the impression that if I had moved my eyes, the eyes of the person in the video would have moved too."* *"It seemed as if the touch I felt was caused by the brush touching the face in the movie."*
    *   **The Inference:** If acting together — in motoric, vocal, or sensory ways — can serve as a **surrogate for being together in a kinship unit**, we should see similar consequences from both forms of togetherness. We do. Two matter most for influence: **enhanced liking** and **greater support** — both achievable pre-suasively.

### CONSEQUENCE 1 — LIKING
*   When people act in unison they not only see themselves as more alike, they **evaluate one another more positively afterward.** Elevated likeness turns into elevated **liking**. The actions can be finger tapping in a laboratory, smiling in a conversation, or body adjustments in a teacher-student interaction — all of which, when synchronized, cause people to rate one another more favorably.
*   **The Racial Prejudice Experiment (Canadian researchers):** The premise: we normally "resonate" (harmonize) with in-group members and typically don't with out-group members; that difference in felt unity may drive automatic in-group favoritism. White subjects watched **seven video clips** of black individuals taking a sip of water and placing the glass down. Some merely observed. Others were asked to **imitate the actions — sipping from a glass in exact coordination with the movements they witnessed.** Later, a procedure measuring **hidden** racial preferences: those who had merely observed showed the typical white favoritism for whites over blacks. **Those who had synchronized their actions with the black actors exhibited none of this favoritism.**
*   **The Honest Caveat:** The change was measured only a few minutes after the unitizing procedure, and the researchers presented no evidence it would persist beyond the time or place of the study. Still, there is room for optimism: a less biased approach can be **all that's necessary within a specific situation** — a job interview, a sales call, a first meeting.

### CONSEQUENCE 2 — SUPPORT (the gold standard: consequential, self-sacrificial behavior)
*   **Study 1 — Aid to a Single Person:** Participants listened to recorded audio tones and tapped a table to the beats. Some heard the **same** tones as their partner (so they saw themselves tapping **in concert**); others heard a **different** array (no synchrony). Afterward, all learned they were free to leave, but their partners had to remain to answer a lengthy series of math and logic problems — **however, they could choose to stay and help by taking on part of the task themselves.**
    *   **Result:** Only **18%** of those who had **not** tapped in synchrony chose to stay and help. Of those who **had** tapped in synchrony, **49%** gave up their free time to assist their partners.
*   **Study 2 — Cooperation With Team Members (the military cohesion tactic):** After assignment to teams, some teams were asked to **walk together, in step**, for a time; others walked together for the same time but **normally**. Then all played an economic game in which they could either maximize their own financial gain or **forgo that opportunity to ensure their teammates would do well financially.**
    *   **Result:** Members of teams that had previously **marched together were 50% more cooperative** toward their teammates.
    *   **The mechanism:** A follow-up study showed it — **preliminary-response synchrony led to a feeling of unity, which led to a greater willingness to sacrifice personal gain for the group's greater good.**
    *   *(Note the dark mirror, from the research literature:* marching in unison is still used in military training even though its battlefield worth vanished long ago — and after marching together, people became more willing to **comply with a fellow marcher's request to harm members of an out-group**, whether the requester was an authority figure or a peer. Unitizing procedures amplify whatever direction the group points.)
*   **The Scalability Problem and Its Answer:** Simultaneous table tapping, water sipping, and face brushing aren't readily implementable at scale; marching in unison is better but only marginally. Isn't there a generally applicable mechanism that social entities can deploy to bring about synchrony and influence members toward group goals? **There is: it's music.**

### MUSIC IN THE STRUGGLE FOR INFLUENCE ("It's a Jingle Out There")
*   **Why Music Syncs:** Because of a unique collection of **detectible regularities** — rhythm, meter, intensity, pulse, and time — music possesses rare synchronizing power. Listeners can easily become aligned with one another along **motoric, sensory, vocal, and emotional** dimensions — a state that leads to familiar markers of unity: **self-other merging, social cohesion, and supportive conduct.**
*   **The Four-Year-Olds (Germany):** As part of a game, some children walked around a circle with a partner while **singing and keeping time with recorded music**; others did nearly the same **without** the musical accompaniment. Later, when given an opportunity to show helpfulness, those who had sung and walked together **in time with music were over three times more likely to help their partner** than those who had no joint musical experience.
    *   **Two instructive points from the authors:** (1) The helping was **self-sacrificial** — it required the helper to give up some personal play time. (2) **The help "wasn't rooted in rationality at all. It was spontaneous, intuitive, and based on an emotional sense of connection"** that naturally accompanies shared musical engagement. That second point has significant implications for managing the influence process.

### SYSTEMS ENGINEERING (System 1 vs. System 2 — and the Matching Rule)
*   **The Distinction (Kahneman):** **System 1** is fast, associative, intuitive, and often emotional. **System 2** is slower, deliberative, analytical, and rational. Support for their separateness: **activating one inhibits the other.** It is difficult to think hard about an occurrence while experiencing it emotionally, and difficult to fully experience it while parsing it logically.
*   **The Matching Rule (an operational law worth memorizing):** *Persuaders would be wise to match the System 1 versus 2 orientation of any appeal to the corresponding orientation of the recipient.*
    *   If a buyer considers a car purchase primarily from the standpoint of **emotionally relevant features** (attractive looks, exhilarating acceleration), use **feelings-related arguments** — even merely saying **"I feel this is the one for you"** will be more successful.
    *   If they consider it primarily on **rational grounds** (fuel economy, trade-in value), **"I think this is the one for you"** would be more likely to close the sale.
*   **Music Is System 1:** In their sensory and visceral responses, people sing, swing, snake, and sway in rhythmic alignment with it — and, if together, with each other. Rarely do they think analytically while music is prominent in consciousness. **Under music's influence, the deliberative, rational route to knowing becomes difficult to access and, hence, largely unavailable.**
    *   **Voltaire (contemptuous):** *"Anything too stupid to be spoken is sung."*
    *   **The Advertising Adage (tactical):** *"If you can't make your case to an audience with facts, **sing** it to them."* Communicators whose ideas have little rational firepower don't have to give up the fight — they can undertake a **flanking maneuver**, moving the campaign to a battleground where rationality possesses little force and where sensations of harmony, synchrony, and unity win.
*   **The Musician Mystery (resolved):** Why are young women so attracted to musicians? "There's no logic to it, right? **Precisely.**" It doesn't matter that the probabilities of a successful relationship with most musicians are notoriously low — those are *rational* probabilities. It doesn't matter that their current and future economic prospects are equally low — those are *economic* reasons. **Music isn't about such practicalities. It's about harmonies — melodic ones that lead to emotional and relational ones.** And music and romance are strongly associated in life: a systematic count found **80%** of contemporary songs have romance as their subject — the vast majority. "Romance isn't at issue the vast majority of the time when we speak or think or write, but it is when we sing."
    *   **The Guitar Case Field Study (France):** A man approached young women and asked for their phone numbers while carrying a **guitar case (31% success)**, a **sports bag (9% success)**, or **nothing (14% success)**.
*   **Armstrong's Correction (the practical caution):** In J. Scott Armstrong's *Persuasive Advertising*, a 2008 analysis found **87% of thirty-second TV commercials incorporated music** — but this routine addition of music to the message "might well be flawed." Armstrong reviewed the relevant research and concluded that **music should be used only to advertise familiar, feelings-based products** (snack foods, body scents) **in an emotional context — that is, where thinking is unlikely.** For products that have **high personal consequences and strong supportive arguments** (safety equipment, software packages) — **where hard thinking is likely and instructive — background music undercuts ad effectiveness.**

### CONTINUING RECIPROCAL EXCHANGE (the 36 Questions)
*   **The Viral Entry Point:** In early 2015 a *New York Times* piece in the **Fashion & Styles** section — "To Fall in Love with Anyone, Do This," by Mandy Len Catron — went viral and became one of the most widely spread *Times* pieces ever, claiming a method for producing the intense emotional closeness and social bonds of love **in the space of forty-five minutes.**
*   **The Underlying Principle:** Other psychologists have demonstrated that **a history of reciprocally exchanged favors leads individuals to give additional favors to their exchange partner, no matter who provided the last one.** This is the correct frame for the Harrisons: they didn't agree to house Skip because they owed a favor. **The process of reciprocal exchange had prompted their assent by creating a relationship rather than an unmet obligation.**
*   **The Procedure (Arthur and Elaine Aron):** In pairs, participants took turns **reading questions to their partner**, who would answer, and who would then **receive their partner's answer to the same item.** Advancing through the **thirty-six questions** required participants to disclose **progressively more personal** information and, in turn, to learn more personal information about their partner.
    *   Early: *"What would constitute a perfect day for you?"*
    *   Later: *"What do you value most in a friendship?"*
    *   Near the end: *"Of all the people in your family, whose death would be the most disturbing?"*
*   **The Result:** Relationships deepened beyond all expectations. The procedure generated feelings of emotional closeness and interpersonal unity **unparalleled within a forty-five-minute span** — especially among complete strangers in an emotionally sterile laboratory setting. Not a chance occurrence: hundreds of studies have since confirmed the effect, and **some participants have even gotten married** as a result.
*   **Elaine Aron's Two Named Keys:** (1) **The items escalate in personal disclosure** — so participants increasingly open themselves up in a trusting way representative of tightly bonded pairs. (2) **Participants do so by acting together** — in a coordinated, back-and-forth fashion, making the interaction **inherently and continuously synchronous.**

### CO-CREATION (the fourth route to unitization)
*   **Aldo Leopold's Birch and Pine:** Leopold — who held the first-ever professorship of wildlife management in the United States, at the University of Wisconsin, principally in the 1930s and 1940s — developed a distinctive environmental ethic in his bestselling *A Sand County Almanac*: it challenged the dominant conservation model (ecologies managed for human usage) and advocated instead for the **right of all plant and animal species to existence in their natural state** whenever possible. With such a clear and heartfelt position, he was more than surprised one day to find himself, **ax in hand, cutting down a red birch tree on his property so one of his white pines would get more light and space.**
    *   He searched for the logic behind his bias and found only one factor he was convinced was primary — one founded entirely on feeling: *"Well, first of all, I planted the pine with my shovel, whereas the birch crawled in under the fence and planted itself. My bias is thus to some extent paternal. . . ."*
*   **The Ikea Effect:** People who have built items themselves come to see *"their amateurish creations as similar in value to experts' creations."*
*   **The Extension to a Co-Creator:** Cialdini notes that Leopold was no lone actor — he was a **co-creator, with nature**, of the mature pine he once put in the ground as a sapling. The intriguing possibility: did he, as a result of acting together with Mother Nature, come to feel **more merged with her** — and even more enamored and respectful of his partner in the collaboration? Leopold has not been available for questioning since 1948. Cialdini is confident of the answer.
*   **THE MANAGERS STUDY (Pfeffer & Cialdini) — the empirical answer, including one striking surprise:**
    *   **Expected finding #1:** Managers led to believe they'd had a **large** role in developing an end product (an ad for a new wristwatch) rated the ad **50% more favorably** than managers led to believe they'd had little developmental involvement — **even though the final ad they saw was identical in all cases.**
    *   **Expected finding #2:** The managers with the greatest perceived involvement **rated themselves more responsible** for the ad's quality, in terms of their much greater perceived managerial control over their employee.
    *   **THE UNEXPECTED FINDING #3:** *"The more the managers attributed the success of the project to themselves, the more they **also** attributed it to the ability of their employee."* Per simple logic, only 100% of personal responsibility can be distributed — if one party's perceived contribution rises, the partner's should fall. It didn't. **The explanation:** *if co-creation causes at least a temporary merging of identities, then what applies to one partner also applies to the other — distributional logic notwithstanding.*
*   **Other Domains Where Co-Creation Diminishes Hard Problems:**
    *   **Children and Sharing:** Children below the age of six or seven are typically **selfish** with rewards, rarely distributing them equally with playmates — **unless they obtained those rewards through a collaborative effort with a playmate, whereupon even three-year-olds share equally the majority of the time.**
    *   **Classrooms:** Students tend to coalesce along racial, ethnic, and socioeconomic lines, finding friends and helpmates mainly within their own groups. **This pattern declines significantly after they've engaged co-creatively with students from other groups within "cooperative learning" exercises, in which each student has to teach a portion of the information to the others so that they can all get a good score.**
    *   **Brands:** Companies struggle to get consumers to feel bonded with — and therefore loyal to — their brands. They've been winning by **inviting current and prospective customers to co-create with them** novel or updated products and services, most often by providing the company with information about desirable features.
*   **THE CRITICAL PHRASING RULE — Advice vs. Opinions/Expectations:** Within such marketing partnerships, **consumer input must be framed as ADVICE to the company, not as OPINIONS ABOUT or EXPECTATIONS FOR the company.**
    *   **Advice** puts a person in a **merging state of mind**, which stimulates a linking of one's own identity with another party's.
    *   **Opinion or expectation** puts a person in an **introspective state of mind**, which involves focusing on oneself.
    *   These only slightly different forms of feedback — and the vitally different merging-versus-separating mindsets they produce — can have a significant impact on consumer engagement with a brand.
*   **THE SPLASH! RESTAURANT SURVEY (the proof):** Online survey takers from around the US were shown a description of the business plan for a new fast-casual restaurant, **Splash!**, that hoped to distinguish itself through the healthfulness of its menu items. After reading, all were asked for feedback — but some were asked for any **"advice"** they might have regarding the restaurant, while others were asked for any **"opinions"** or **"expectations."** Finally, they indicated how likely they'd be to patronize a Splash! restaurant.
    *   **Result:** Participants who provided **advice** reported wanting to eat at a Splash! **significantly more** than participants who provided either of the other sorts of feedback.
    *   **The mediating mechanism:** Just as we'd expect if giving advice is indeed a mechanism of unitization, **the increased desire to support the restaurant came from feeling more linked with the brand.**
    *   **THE CLINCHER:** Participants **rated all three types of feedback as equally helpful** to the restaurateurs. So it wasn't that advice-givers felt connected because they thought they had aided the brand more. Instead, **having to give advice put participants in a togetherness state of mind rather than a separateness state of mind just before they had to reflect on what they would say about the brand** — a finding Cialdini admits pleases him because it implicates the **pre-suasive** character of the process.
*   **The Face-to-Face Recommendation (and the objection demolished):** This set of results clinches the wisdom — and, if done in an authentic search for useful information, the **ethicality** — of **asking for advice** in face-to-face interactions with friends, colleagues, and customers. It should even prove effective in interactions with **superiors.**
    *   **The reasonable worry:** asking a boss for advice might make you come off as incompetent, dependent, or insecure.
    *   **Why the worry is mistaken:** As the study of supervisors' estimation of collaborators' contributions indicated, the effects of co-creation are **not well captured by reason, rationality, or logic.** They are, however, **exceedingly well captured by a particular socially promotive feeling in the situation: the (highly beneficial for you) state of togetherness.**
    *   **Saul Bellow:** *"When we ask for advice, we are usually looking for an accomplice."* Cialdini adds, on the basis of scientific evidence: **if we get that advice, we usually get that accomplice** — "and what better abettor to have on a project than someone in charge?"
*   **GETTING TOGETHER (the chapter's closing question):** It's time to look past the mostly favorable consequences of being together and acting together. We learned we can pre-suasively solidify support from a company's shareholders as well as its customers, help ensure soldiers stand and fight rather than flee, arrange for playmates, classmates, and workmates to like, help, and cooperate with one another, get nearly any parent to fill out a long survey with no financial compensation, and even produce love in a lab. **But the unanswered question: can the lessons apply to much larger stages — age-old international enmities, violent religious clashes, simmering racial antagonisms? Could what we know about being together and acting together increase our chance of getting together, as a species?**
    *   Cialdini believes procedures that establish a **pre-suasive feeling of unity** establish a context for desirable change — and provided that **both parties share the feeling**, the change is likely to be **mutual**, reducing the probability that either party will feel exploited, deepening a sense of togetherness, and raising the likelihood of continued positive interaction.
    *   But he warns it "would be naïve, owing to the attendant procedural and cultural complications, to presume that the theory would work out smoothly in practice" — the specifics would have to be optimally designed and enacted with those complexities in mind, "something with which the experts on the issues would surely agree and which might be the worthy subject of an entire follow-up book."
    *   *His own self-deprecating guard against overreach:* he invokes the biologist Steve Jones's observation that senior scientists often begin to "boom about Big Issues," acting as if specialized knowledge licenses confident pronouncements far outside their sphere — and admits that at the end of this chapter, **"plainly, I'd be booming in the dark."**
## CHAPTER 13: ETHICAL USE — A PRE-PRE-SUASIVE CONSIDERATION
*   **The Framing:** A central assertion of the book is that what we say or do **immediately before** an appeal significantly affects its success. But there is a related choice that occurs even before that one: **whether, on ethical grounds, to try to attain success in that way at all.** This is the *pre-pre-suasive* decision.
*   **The Charge Against the Book:** Media questioners repeatedly pressed the "more harm than good" argument — that by revealing the secrets of social influence, Cialdini gives unethical practitioners the means to trick consumers into buying even more of whatever they're peddling.
*   **Why the Old Defenses Don't Work Here:**
    *   With *Influence*, two defenses held: (1) it was written **for consumers**, to recognize and reject unfair influence attempts; and (2) the material came **from the practitioners** — so it wasn't handing out new techniques, it was balancing the equation for consumers.
    *   With *Pre-Suasion*, **neither defense is available.** The conclusions are principally about how to **harness** influence rather than deflect it. And the practice of pre-suasion is **not widely employed** within the community of influence professionals — only a rarified few understand the processes well enough to commission them systematically. So this time, publication genuinely could enlighten unethical organizations. And the concern deepens because **many pre-suasive processes operate unconsciously and therefore go unrecognized.**
*   **The Traditional Case Against Deceit — Reputational Damage:**
    *   The argument: deceptive tactics might increase profit short-term, but **once exposed** produce unacceptably high costs — damage to reputation, trust, and future earnings.
    *   **Why it's a *smart* argument for business audiences:** it makes its case on the dimension of **economic vitality**, which leaders must weigh to maintain growth and survival — a more motivating rationale in a commercial environment than "finger wagging about a lack of virtue." *("I'm sure there will never be a law against shouting 'Ethics!' in a crowded boardroom, because — unlike 'Fire!' in a crowded theater — the term doesn't possess enough mobilizing force there to spur urgent movement.")*
    *   **The Evidence Is Solid:** A 2005 study of **585 firms** subjected to SEC enforcement proceedings for financial misrepresentation found companies lost an average of **41% of their market value** after the misconduct was publicized — and **nearly two-thirds of that loss stemmed from reputational harm.** **80% of Americans** say their perception of a company's ethicality directly affects their decisions to purchase its goods or services. After Volkswagen's diesel emissions trickery became known in 2015, its sales dropped to **one-sixteenth of the industry average**, it suffered the **largest annual loss in company history**, and its reputation among vehicle owners went from **70% favorable to 80% NOT favorable.** And the damage is **singularly difficult to undo**: research indicates a disreputable company attempting to recover lost trust would need to demonstrate newfound integrity **consistently and on many occasions** to convince the wary — a recovery that could take **years**, by which time former customers have established links to rivals.
*   **The Uncomfortable Data That Broke the Old Argument:** A pair of global surveys revealed that **senior business leaders are very much aware** of the reputational case — and that uncomfortably large numbers of them are **willing to undertake misconduct anyway.** Despite understanding the risks, **close to half of high-ranking executives reported they would act unethically to get or retain business.** Sales and marketing staffers, most likely to endorse ethically dubious conduct, were **least likely to be questioned about it** by the company. Employees of these firms saw **few actions taken at the top** either to prevent profit-enhancing ethical violations or to punish those involved afterward. Result: the amount of unethical activity in commercial organizations remains distressingly high.
*   **Why Deterrence Fails (the diagnosis):** It's conceivable leaders are compartmentalizing — mentally walling off knowledge of reputational risk from active participation in or tacit permission of misconduct. Cialdini doesn't buy it: *"Those at the highest levels of an organization don't get to their prominent positions by systematically ignoring clear and present dangers."* His simpler explanation: **they just don't expect to get caught.** They wouldn't engage in misconduct if they thought it would become visible to penalty-wielding customers, clients, and regulators. This fits crime-deterrence research: individuals who commit violations carrying significant negative consequences **do not believe they will be caught.**
*   **The Dilemma:** Many leaders *would* be dissuaded by alarming economic considerations — but the cautionary economic argument has failed because **it depends on an expectation of discovery**, which perpetrators don't possess when deciding to act.

### THE TRIPLE-TUMOR STRUCTURE OF ORGANIZATIONAL DISHONESTY
*   **The Thesis (Cialdini, Jessica Li & Adriana Samper):** An organization that regularly approves, encourages, or allows deceitful tactics in its **external** dealings (with customers, clients, stockholders, suppliers, distributors, regulators) will experience a nasty set of **internal** consequences akin to tumors. They will be **malignant** — growing, spreading, progressively eating at the organization's health and vigor — and they will be **difficult to trace and identify via typical accounting methods** as the true causes of inferior profitability, leading to expensive, misguided efforts that fail to target the genuine culprits.
*   **The Three Tumors:** **poor employee performance, high employee turnover, and prevalent employee fraud and malfeasance.** The claim is not about localized or infrequent ethical infractions, but about a **dominant organizational culture** that permits or promotes dishonest business practices.
*   *"Do not seek dishonest gains; dishonest gains are losses." — Hesiod*

### TUMOR 1 — POOR EMPLOYEE PERFORMANCE
*   **The Cost Baseline:** Occupational stress when combined leads annually to about **120,000 deaths and $200 billion in additional health care expenses in the United States alone**, where employers bear much of the burden. (The researchers argued that workplace stress's effect on health outcomes is comparable to **secondhand tobacco smoke** — and that just as organizations took steps to reduce exposure to smoke, they should reduce exposure to management practices that create excessive stress.)
*   **Moral Stress (the specific killer):** It comes from **a conflict between an employee's ethical values and the perceived ethical values of the organization** — and it can be *more* harmful than other stressors known to be highly damaging.
    *   **The Call-Center Study:** Customer service agents at a financial services call center were compared on moral stress versus difficult customers, lack of supervisor/coworker support, conflicting task demands, and dead-end job descriptions. **Only moral stress predicted both of two performance-degrading outcomes: employee fatigue** (low emotional spirit and physical energy) **and job burnout** (loss of enthusiasm for and interest in one's work). Each is a serious managerial problem alone; combined they are a managerial nightmare, "robbing workers of the vigor, desire, and capacity to do their jobs well." The activities that most produced moral stress were those requiring employees to be **dishonest with customers** in order to perform their job duties.
*   **The Experiment (does merely observing dishonesty hurt the observer?):**
    *   Business students sat at computers networked to "team members" at various universities, told their team would compete on problem-solving tasks against other teams nationwide, and that a first-task win would give their team a competitive advantage on the next group task. Because of a "technology issue" with only their computer, they couldn't send information to teammates — but could see their teammates' online interactions.
    *   **Condition A (deceptive):** After the first task, the team leader said the team had gotten **67%** correct but intended to **report 80%** to improve perceived performance — "because there was no way that cheating could be discovered by the researcher." None of the other members objected.
    *   **Condition B (honest):** Identical, except the leader reported intending to submit the honest 67%. Again, no objections from teammates.
    *   **The Result:** All participants then took an individual critical-reasoning test drawn from a well-validated business intelligence instrument. **Those whose work team had previously been deceptive scored 20% lower** than the others. And the reason was visible: **after working on the problem for a while, the deceptive-team members just stopped — significantly sooner than the others** — suggesting they lacked the same energy or motivation to continue.
    *   **The Anticipated Objection (and the answer):** Lab experiment, university students, artificially constructed environment — in truth "the most frustrating feature to us of these imagined critics was that they had a point." So they ran a **national survey of adults** who had been at their current or most recent job for an average of **three years**, measuring (a) the ethical climate of the organization as set by its managers and leaders, (b) the amount of stress they felt there, and (c) the quality of their work performance.
    *   **The Survey Results (which fit and even exceeded the lab findings):** (1) the more unethical the climate, the **poorer** the workers' job performance; (2) the more unethical the climate, the **more stress** they felt at work; and (3) **that particular stress caused their poor performance.** *"Strike one."*

### TUMOR 2 — EMPLOYEE TURNOVER
*   **The Cost Structure:** Turnover costs can be assessed fairly precisely — direct expenses (severance, recruitment, hiring, training the replacement) run from **50% of annual compensation for lower-level positions to over 200% of total compensation for executive-level positions.** Indirect hits spiral higher: loss of institutional memory, sales and productivity disruptions, lowered morale among remaining team members. A conservative estimate: assume combined direct and indirect expenses equal just **one year's total remuneration**. US voluntary turnover now exceeds **15% per year.** For a midsize firm of a thousand employees, even if only **10%** walked (averaging $40,000 in total salary and benefits), they would take **$4 million every year** in turnover costs.
*   **The Link to Ethics:** The mechanism is **moral stress** — one reason an honest person leaves a work unit is if staying means having to be a party to presiding duplicity.
*   **The Experiment:** Business students, again split into teams with a leader who either advanced the agenda through **deception** or did not. Afterward, before a second group task, they could elect either to **stay with their team or move to a different one.**
    *   **Result:** **51% of those in the ethical group decided to leave their team; 80% of those in the unethical group chose to leave theirs.**
*   **The Survey Confirmation:** Employees of unethically rated organizations were more likely to feel stressed and more likely to want to quit — and **it was this stress that was pushing them to leave**, leaving their employers with financially draining turnover costs as a consequence. *"Strike two."*

### TUMOR 3 — EMPLOYEE FRAUD AND MALFEASANCE
*   **The Mechanism (the most counterintuitive and most valuable insight in the chapter):** *"Those who cheat for you will cheat against you. If you encourage the first form of deceit, you will get the second, which will cost you dearly in the bargain."*
*   **Why:** The exodus is launched by the stress from conflicting moral values, so it is **specific to employees with high ethical standards.** Those comfortable with the use of trickery to achieve financial gains should be **happy to stay.** A company with an enduring culture of misconduct therefore pushes away many honest employees, **leaving an accumulating precipitate of individuals disposed toward wrongdoing** — who, when given the opportunity, **will do their wrong to the firm.** By this argument such an enterprise "will draw under its coat a viper — a snake whose bite will be fiscally venomous," as worldwide costs of employee fraud and malfeasance (embezzlement, theft of inventory or equipment, expense account inflation, falsified purchasing reports, under-the-table deals with vendors or business partners) figure in **trillions of dollars** — and these losses are **rarely recouped.**
*   **The Experiment (the clincher):**
    *   Recall that in the second study, some participants chose to leave their team and some chose to stay. At this point, everyone was told that due to unforeseen logistical difficulties, **team switching wasn't possible** — so they'd work on the next task with their original teammates.
    *   Actually, the next task required them to work **against** those teammates to determine who could best solve an array of word problems quickly; whoever did so within one minute would increase the probability of winning a **$100 prize.**
    *   Before participants provided their solutions, the researchers arranged for them to **"accidentally" see the answer key in an untraceable way.** Pretests had established that the typical student at the university could solve **3.17** problems in a minute — so comparing reported problems solved against that standard revealed who was cheating to elevate their own economic interests over their colleagues'.
    *   **The Results, by group:**
        *   Participants who had been part of an **honest** work unit and **elected to stay in it**: cheating was **nonexistent to negligible** — "a high five should be given to the many leaders who have had the integrity and organizational wisdom to establish an ethical culture."
        *   Participants choosing to **leave** either an honest or a dishonest work unit: cheated somewhat, but **not to a statistically significant degree.**
        *   **THE BIG NEWS:** those who, when previously given the chance, had **opted to remain in a dishonest environment** — **cheated 77% more than the average of everyone else.** And their cheating "didn't merely enhance their own financial prospects but also **undermined those of the people around them.**"
*   **The Survey Confirmation:** They examined relationships among employees' ratings of their workplace's ethicality as set by leaders, their desire to leave, and their **admissions of malfeasance** on the job (altering expense reports, sabotaging equipment to avoid having to work, using company resources for private purposes). **The key finding: employees in unethical organizations who preferred to remain in them were abnormally likely to engage in such deceitful, financially costly workplace activity.** *"Just as in our laboratory experiment, individuals happy to reside in unethical workplace nests were revealed to be happy to foul those nests."*
*   **The MBA Student's Essay (a real-world illustration):** One student described a company he'd worked for that started healthy and had since suffered a **$1 billion loss**: *"The CEO abused the influence principles on a regular basis: he claimed scarcity when there was abundance, he used his authority to make others act against their better judgment, and created examples of social proof that didn't exist. People believed him in the short run, but as the truth leaked out, the company's reputation deteriorated. Few companies are willing to do business with him now — those that do will only do so on onerous terms. The culture of dishonesty trickled down throughout the organization. The marketing department was coerced to exaggerate the truth, the PR department wrote mostly false press releases, and salespeople coerced customers. Job dissatisfaction and turnover were incredibly high. People were attracted to the company by high salaries... but left as soon as they could find work elsewhere. Taking a cue from the executives, employees would steal from the company whenever they could, usually via travel and expense reports. Some would cut side deals with suppliers. When I visited a few months ago, nearly half the staff had left, and morale was lower than ever."*
    *   *"Strike three"?*
*   **What Leaders Can Do (the three recommendations):**
    *   Cialdini hopes so, but warns there is **no Tinker Bell scenario**: unless leaders set the proper organizational tone, the argument won't take wing just because the rest of us really, really believe in it. **The concurrence of those at the top is required.** Fortunately, if they want, they can build and maintain an ethically commendable workplace culture as readily as an ethically objectionable one. For one large group of leaders, no shift is needed — they've already decided on that route and should be admired for following it. For the nearly half who feel economic considerations warrant dishonest conduct, it may be necessary to lay out the countervailing costs (employee underperformance, turnover, and malfeasance) that apply **even when the misconduct is not detected by outsiders.**
    *   **The Three Recommended Organizational Structures:**
        1.  **Honesty ratings** by customers and clients of employees they interact with should be part of those employees' **incentive structures.**
        2.  The **ethical reputation of the company as a whole** should be measured and included in assessments of **yearly performance.**
        3.  **Ratings by employees of the firm's ethical orientation** should be a component of **senior management's — and especially the CEO's — compensation package.**
    *   **Why these work (and the pre-suasive logic):** The effect would not only incentivize ethical conduct but also bring **continuing, heightened attention** to ethical standards — and, per chapters 3 and 4, "the consequent focused attention on these matters would (justifiably) elevate their perceived importance and causal role in organizational affairs."

## CHAPTER 14: POST-SUASION — AFTEREFFECTS
*   **The Problem:** Successful pre-suasion works by channeling attention **temporarily** to a favorable concept. So when rival communicators or everyday events divert that attention, what prevents the favorability from evaporating? Time-limited shifts can be extraordinarily productive when managed astutely — but for optimal impact the hope is **enduring effect.** Two strategies are available: an old-school one and a new-school one.

### STRATEGY 1 (OLD SCHOOL) — INSTALL STRONG COMMITMENTS
*   **The Rule:** Arrange for the individual to make a **commitment** to the affirmative response, usually in the form of an **active step.**
*   **The Medical No-Show Study (Steve J. Martin, British medical clinics):** The standard practice — calling patients the day before to remind them — reduced failures to appear by **3.5%**, required time and money, and didn't always reach its targets. Compare the **commitment procedure**: when making a future appointment after an office visit, the receptionist normally writes the time and date on a card and hands it to the patient. **If instead the patients are asked to fill in the card themselves, that active step commits them.** The costless change dropped the subsequent no-show rate by **18%.** (An 18% decline could net the equivalent of **$180 million per year in the United Kingdom alone.**)
*   **The American Flag Study (2008 election, Obama vs. McCain):**
    *   Americans in several states were surveyed online about political attitudes and beliefs. **Half saw a small American flag at the top left corner of the survey questionnaire; half saw no flag.**
    *   The subtle flag exposure made participants **more favorable to McCain's Republican Party and its politically conservative ideology.**
    *   When resurveyed after the election, those who had encountered the flag **voted for McCain at a significantly greater rate.**
    *   **And, most remarkably: a full eight months after the election**, participants who had made contact with the flag during that long-past survey were **still embracing more Republican Party–oriented attitudes, beliefs, and judgments.**
    *   **The Two-Process Explanation:** (1) **Pre-suasive:** background exposure to the flag put participants in mind of Republican Party thinking (a pilot study confirmed Americans reliably made that link in 2008) — just as French background music temporarily disposed shoppers toward French wine, and clouds disposed furniture-site visitors toward soft things. (2) **Commitment-based:** after coming across the flag and becoming temporarily more inclined toward Republicanism, participants were asked to **act on that inclination by registering it in the survey** — thereby committing to it behaviorally. That commitment led to an even more committing form of activity when they **subsequently voted**, which in turn led to a **solidified** Republican orientation when measured eight months later.
    *   **The Parallel Artwork Study:** After people read a happy story, their temporarily elevated mood caused them to like a painting. **Five days later, only those who had actively rated it while in that elated state still felt the same way about it.** Those who had not rated it — and therefore had not "locked in" that evaluation behaviorally — showed **no special preference at all** once the happy mood had dissipated.
*   **The Implication:** *Pre-suasive openers can produce dramatic, immediate shifts in people, but to turn those shifts into durable changes, it's necessary to get commitments to them, usually in the form of related behavior.*
*   **What Makes a Commitment Durable:** The most effective commitments **reach into the future by incorporating behaviors that affect one's personal identity.** They do so by ensuring the commitment is undertaken in an **active, effortful, and voluntary** fashion — because each of these elements communicates **deep personal preferences.**
    *   **Worked example:** If a pre-suasive maneuver (images of people standing together) makes me temporarily inclined toward an inclusive social policy like raising the minimum wage, and I see myself acting on that preference by making a quickly requested financial contribution, I become more committed on the spot. If the action was both **freely chosen** (entirely my choice) and **difficult or costly** (a substantial donation), I'm even more likely to view it as **indicating what I favor as a person.** That behaviorally influenced **self-perception** anchors and aligns my later responding on the issue — even though the whole sequence was instigated pre-suasively by a momentary shift of attention to the concept of togetherness.
    *   **The publicness caveat (a refinement on the older literature):** Good evidence exists that **public** (more than private) initial commitments to a cause reach extensively into the future. But newer evidence indicates this is the case **principally when individuals already feel a strong personal connection to the cause.** If that personal connection is **not** present, a **private** commitment becomes the superior vehicle for delivering lasting change.

### STRATEGY 2 (NEW SCHOOL) — CUEING UP THE CUES
*   **The Pyramid Scheme Bus (the origin observation):** Cialdini answered a mail invitation to "an amazing opportunity to achieve wealth, prosperity, and financial independence beyond your dreams." About fifty people gathered at a Phoenix restaurant on a Saturday morning — then were escorted onto aging, distinctively colored yellow-and-blue buses for a **two-hour highway drive to Tucson**, where the "educational event" was to happen. At lunch in Tucson a speaker **briefly reviewed the points we'd heard on the bus ride.** That was it. Why spend good money on a bewildering journey?
    *   **Cialdini's epiphany:** Halfway to Tucson he looked out his window and saw **another aging, yellow-and-blue bus carrying riders from Tucson to Phoenix.** The program information was never envisioned for Tucson. **It had always been designed for the bus.**
    *   **The two psychological reasons:** (1) **It's hard to think hard on a rolling, noisy, bumpy, crowded, emotionally agitating bus** — "and hard thinking is the chief foe of pyramid sales systems." (2) **When people can't deliberate carefully or concentrate fully, they are much more likely to respond automatically to whatever decision-making cues are present in the situation.** On the bus, the organizers **controlled those cues**: achievement-related posters on walls and ceiling, wealth-linked slogans taped to seatbacks, success-themed music before each speaker (Rocky soundtracks; "Eye of the Tiger" a favorite). Speaker messages: *"You can do it, you can do it, you can do it, you can do it — provided you use the system."* Supporting cues: an expensive beautifully tailored suit, an **$11,000 commission check** "from this month alone," a glowing testimonial letter from someone who before the program had "been just like you folks." **By the time we'd returned to Phoenix, two-thirds of us had signed on.**
*   **The Modern Parallel and the Prescription:** Modern life is becoming more like that bus — **speedy, turbulent, stimulus saturated, and mobile.** We are all becoming less able to think hard and well about what best to do in many situations, so even the most careful-minded are increasingly likely to **react automatically to the cues for action that exist in those settings.**
    *   **Are we fated to be bozos on this bus?** Not if, rather than raging against the invading automaticity, **we invite it in but take systematic control of the way it operates on us.** *"We have to become interior designers of our regular living spaces, furnishing them with features that will send us unthinkingly in the directions we most want to go in those spaces."*
*   **The Self-Engineering Playbook (arranging for initial preferences to guide future action by ensuring we regularly encounter cues that automatically activate them):**
    *   To write for a particular audience: **surround yourself with cues linked to the group — for instance, typical audience members' faces.**
    *   To approach a task with a strong **achievement orientation**: give yourself contact with images of success, striving, and accomplishment — **a runner winning a race.**
    *   To approach a task with an **analytical orientation** (e.g., figuring a budget): give yourself access to images of contemplation, thoughtfulness, and examination — **Rodin's *The Thinker*.**
    *   **Optimize multiple task types in the same place on the same computer by changing the desktop wallpaper** to show a series of images appropriate to whichever orientation you want to apply to a specific task.
    *   **If/when-then plans** provide yet another way to harness associative connections for long-term advantage — by associating desirable goals and actions with cues we will experience in **regularly occurring future situations**: *"If, after my business lunches, the server asks for dessert choices, then I will order mint tea,"* or *"When it's eight in the morning, and I've finished brushing my teeth, then I'll take my prescribed dose of medicine."*

### PERSUASIVE GEOGRAPHIES 2 — WHO WE ARE IS WHERE WE ARE
*   **The Physician Problem:** Asked which people in the health care system are most difficult to influence, health care management groups invariably and emphatically answer: **"Physicians!"** On one hand, that seems right — years of training, specialization, internship, and residency give them deep information and experience to base choices on, making them understandably reluctant to be swayed. On the other hand, this resistance is problematic when physicians don't adopt recommendations that would benefit patients. Which raises a larger issue: **why do most doctors enter medicine in the first place — for other-serving or self-serving reasons?** To benefit and relieve patients' suffering, or to obtain the considerable authority, respect, status, and income that accompany the role?
*   **THE HAND-WASHING STUDY (Grant & Hofmann) — one word, a 45% swing:**
    *   **The problem:** Even though hand washing is strongly recommended before each patient examination, **most physicians wash their hands less than half as often as guidelines prescribe** — and various interventions aimed at reducing the problem had proven ineffective, leaving doctors and patients at greater risk of infection.
    *   **The intervention:** Signs above some examination-room soap or gel dispensers read **"Hand hygiene protects YOU from catching diseases."** Signs over a different set of dispensers read **"Hand hygiene protects PATIENTS from catching diseases."**
    *   **The Result:** The self-focused sign had **no effect** on soap and gel use. The **patient-focused sign increased usage by 45%.**
    *   **The Two Inferences:** (1) Although many other kinds of interventions had failed, **simply drawing doctors' initial attention to the connection between hand washing and patient protection** was a notable success. Of course, in the previous instances when doctors hadn't washed, a deep concern for patient welfare was present in them then too, as was a recognized link between hand washing and patient welfare — **but nothing was there to direct attention to the link, to elevate it to top of mind above the other relevant features of the situation** (how the patient looks, what the nurse is saying, what the notes say). One sign visible on entry was all it took. (2) The data suggests most physicians are **(a) other-oriented individuals, strongly motivated to enhance patients' well-being, and (b) not the sort we'd worry would serve their own interests at patients' expense.**
*   **THE GIFT-TAKING STUDY (Carnegie Mellon) — which casts doubt on the second inference and reveals the real mechanism:**
    *   **The problem:** Physicians are frequently offered gifts from health industry companies (pizza for staff, lunches and dinners, all-expenses-paid trips to conferences, research and consulting contracts, fees for teaching or speaking, even fees for product-related conference calls). **The evidence is strong that these gifts and sponsorships influence physicians to return the favors by prescribing or endorsing products that further the welfare of the industry sponsors rather than of their patients.** Despite that evidence and accompanying warnings, many continue to accept the patient-compromising bounty of their positions — **even while recognizing its problematic implications.**
    *   **The apparent contradiction:** How could physicians act more on behalf of patients' interests than their own in the hand-washing study, yet the reverse in gift taking? One explanation is that hand washing is a relatively **small-cost act** compared with rejecting industry-sponsored benefits, and within higher-value choices self-interest wins out. Maybe. But the rule for reciprocation offers a less cynical, more textured view.
    *   **The Study Design (301 resident physicians):**
        *   **Sample 1 (baseline):** Asked whether and to what extent taking gifts and payments from industry representatives was acceptable. **21.7%** found the practice acceptable.
        *   **Sample 2:** Same question, **preceded by items inquiring into how much they had sacrificed personally and financially to become MDs.** Now **47.5%** thought gift taking acceptable.
        *   **Sample 3:** Reminded of their prior sacrifices **and** asked whether the resources they'd previously expended **justified** taking gifts. Now a clear majority — **60.3%** — came to see the practice as acceptable.
    *   **Cialdini's Multiple Conclusions:** (1) The heartening one — despite a healthy (unhealthy is probably the better word) minority who thought gift taking acceptable, most physicians found it an unworthy choice. (2) **Focusing physicians pre-suasively on the large inputs they had supplied to the health care system made them much more willing to take large returns from it** — implicating the **temporarily surfaced rule for reciprocation** as a major culprit. (3) The answer to whether MDs are primarily patient-serving or self-serving is **"...yes. They are each, depending on their attentional focus at the time."** And that conclusion applies to far more than physicians — their remarkably elastic preferences serve to illustrate **how top-of-mind factors operate on all of us.**
*   **THE BOOK'S FINAL SENTENCE:** *"In large measure, who we are with respect to any choice is where we are, attentionally, in the moment before the choice."* We can be channeled to that privileged moment by (a) choice-relevant **cues we haphazardly bump into** in our daily settings; or (b) of greater concern, by the cues **a knowing communicator has tactically placed** there; or (c) — to much better and lasting effect — by the cues **we have stored in those recurring sites** to send us consistently in desired directions. In each case, **the made moment is pre-suasive.** Whether we are wary of the underlying process, attracted to its potential, or both, we'd be right to acknowledge its considerable power and wise to understand its inner workings.
## THE DMA OPERATOR'S PLAYBOOK — PRE-SUASION APPLIED TO DIRECT RESPONSE
*   **The One-Line Doctrine:** In direct response, we obsess over the **offer** and the **copy**. Pre-suasion says the highest-leverage move is often the **moment before** the copy. Same offer, same proof, same price — engineered differently before contact and the conversion rate moves.
*   **The Founding Insight in Marketing Terms:** *The highest achievers spent more time crafting what they did and said before making a request.* They did not rely on the legitimate merits of the offer to get it accepted. They recognized that **the psychological frame an appeal is first placed in can carry equal or even greater weight** than the offer itself.

### 1. THE OPENERS WE CAN ENGINEER (matched to goal)
*   **Want a higher perceived price / better anchor:** have them write down or acknowledge a **much larger number first**. (The consultant's accidental "I'm not going to be able to charge you a million dollars" line effectively ended all price resistance.)
*   **Want a specific-feature preference (comfort, speed, durability, price):** change the **background** — wallpaper, imagery, set dressing. Clouds → comfort. Pennies → price. Runner winning → achievement. The Thinker → careful assessment. **Audience faces → audience-alignment.**
*   **Want them to try something new:** ask the single-chute identity question first — **"Do you consider yourself adventurous / someone who likes to try new things?"** Then make the ask.
*   **Want them to help / opt in / participate:** ask **"Do you consider yourself a helpful person?"** first. (29% → 77.3%.)
*   **Want them to buy the popular option:** put them in a **threat/wary** state first (crime drama, horror, news content, fear-based content). Want them to buy the **differentiated/premium** option: put them in an **amorous/promiscuous** state first (romance, aspirational content). *Match the ad theme to the programming/feed context.*
*   **Want them to create an account, book a call, give an email:** pre-suade with **unity cues** — faces, shared identity, "of us" markers, language matching.
*   **Want them to buy a bundle/stack:** pre-suade with **unitary evaluation** — a branded quiz, audit, or scorecard that rates **your** offer on its own, with no side-by-side competitor comparison in the same frame.

### 2. THE THREE ATTENTION LEVERS (and the money in each)
*   **Lever 1 — Change what is important (Ch. 3):** Whatever you make focal gets stamped as important *before* they evaluate it. So **choose the one feature you can win on and put the entire frame on it.** The double-barreled effect: they see one-sided evidence on it, *and* they pre-assign it outsized significance.
    *   *Tactical:* the "How are we doing?" review request is not service recovery — it is **single-chute evaluation** with a hidden bonus (favorable scrutiny with no rival scrutiny).
    *   *Tactical:* single-option audits and diagnostic quizzes produce the same effect at scale.
*   **Lever 2 — Change what is causal (Ch. 4):** What is focal is judged to **cause** outcomes. So in any before/after, testimonial, dashboard, or screenshot, make **your mechanism** the most visually prominent element. In video, **find the camera angle and move the chair** — put your offer at the center of the frame and your competitor over your shoulder. (This is why side-by-side comparison creatives are so dangerous to the challenger brand and so potent for the incumbent.)
*   **Lever 3 — Change the task (Ch. 3c):** Give the audience a **task** and you set the agenda. An embedded-reporter-style assignment ("we're going behind the scenes to see how it's actually built") makes the audience evaluate **execution**, not justification. *"Change the task someone is on, and you change what they consider important — without ever arguing the point."*
    *   *Operationally:* swap "Why is our method better?" (molar, invites comparison) for "Here's what a day inside the method looks like" (molecular, invites absorption).

### 3. THE MAGNETIZERS — HOW TO MAKE ATTENTION STICK
*   **Self-relevance (with the necessary caveat):** Use **"you," "your," "your business," "your case"** — not "people," "they," "most marketers." But only after the case is strong. **Self-relevant openers followed by weak proof backfire**, because you have just made an attentive audience scrutinize a thin argument.
*   **The Zeigarnik/Loop Engine:** *Open a loop and do not close it before the ask.*
    *   End short-form video, emails, and ad copy **mid-thought or before the reveal.**
    *   Writers: **never end a writing session at the end of a paragraph or thought** — end mid-sentence and you will return impatient to write.
    *   Television and radio producers have used this for decades: pose a provocative question before the break, answer it only after.
*   **The Mystery Frame (the six-step template) — the single most reliable structure for teaching and for intricate offers:** Pose the mystery → deepen it → home in by killing alternatives → provide a clue → resolve → draw the implication. Use it for VSLs, case-study content, and sales page leads. It is the mechanism that makes people **want the boring details** — the exact opposite of what most copy does.
    *   **Corollary lesson for our own positioning:** *Counterarguments are typically more powerful than arguments*, so one of the best ways to increase acceptance of your message is to **reduce the availability of strong counterarguments** — by pre-empting the objection inside the frame, by removing the competitive comparison, or by winning the frame before the objection can be formed.

### 4. THE ASSOCIATION LAYER — THE WORDS AND THE PHYSICAL PROPS
*   **Vocabulary audit (do this on every sales page and funnel):**
    *   Kill violence words: no **bullet points** (→ information points), no **attack** (→ approach), no **target** (→ goal), no **beat** (→ outdistance/outpace). Keep achievement words — they *raise* performance — but strip them of menace.
    *   Kill loss words: no **cost** or **price** (→ purchase / investment amount).
    *   Kill wear-out words: no **used** (→ preowned).
    *   Kill death words: no **final** destination, no **terminal** (→ gate / destination).
    *   The most damaging concept in human evaluation is **untrustworthiness**. Anything that smells like lying or cheating poisons not just this deal but the relationship.
*   **Metaphor is a meta-door — choose the metaphor whose *natural handling* is your offer:**
    *   **Beast** → catch and cage (system, enforcement, done-for-you control).
    *   **Virus** → remove the conditions that breed it (root-cause, environment, systems redesign).
    *   **Walked out, walked in** → the money fills the gap you leave (Ben Feldman's insurance framing: not death but a breach in responsibility).
    *   *The one-word effect in the crime study was 22% — more than double the effect of gender or party affiliation.* Metaphor is not decoration. It is the highest-yield word choice on the page.
*   **Physical/contextual props that fire the same associations without words:**
    *   **Weight → importance and seriousness.** A weighty binder, a heavy kit, a substantial package, a leather-bound report. This is an argument **against** making every digital asset weightless and frictionless.
    *   **Warmth → trust, closeness, giving.** The physical environment of the sales interaction matters. It is also a reason a live call beats a cold email.
    *   **Cognitive ease → truth and value.** Easy-to-pronounce names, rhyme, clean typography, legible fonts, well-lit menus. **Caution:** hard-to-process forms do signal "think hard" and can increase depth of processing — so use disfluency deliberately, not accidentally.
*   **Implicit egoism (cheap, potent, underused):** Shared birthdays, birthplaces, first names, initials, and — at scale — **name personalization.** "Share a Coke" worked on 100 million UK packs. If your audience is named, **name the thing after them.**

### 5. THE SEVEN CONCEPTS, PRE-SUASIVELY PLACED (not just inside the copy, but in the moment BEFORE it)
*   **Reciprocation:** Give first — and make the give **meaningful, unexpected, and customized.** (Chocolate tips: 3.3% → 14.1% → 21.3%. Yogurt vs. keychain: 24% vs. 12%.) *The customized favor is worth more than the other two combined.* Pre-suade by giving the thing that matches their **current** need, before you ask.
*   **Liking:** The old rule is wrong. It's not "get them to like you" — it's **"show them you genuinely like them."** People trust that those who like them will steer them correctly. Use **language-style matching** (double tips, better negotiations, better hostage outcomes) and **real compliments** (37% tip lift). *"People don't care how much you know until they know how much you care."*
*   **Social Proof:** Use it on **both** vectors. **Validity** ("most people like you do this") and — the underrated one — **feasibility** ("people like you *can* do this"). The 3.5x energy-savings result happened because social proof destroyed the uncertain-achievability problem. In marketing, the most underused use of social proof is **"ordinary people just like you have done this."**
*   **Authority:** **Lead with the weakness.** This is the most immediately deployable tactic in the book. Name the flaw, the limitation, the mistake, the thing they'll find out anyway — up front — **then** bridge with *but / however / yet* to the strength that **undercuts the relevance of that weakness.** (Elizabeth I: body of a weak and feeble woman, *but* the heart of a king. Avis: "We're #2. We try harder.") Also: **label your experts as experts.** A realty receptionist's single added sentence — "our expert agent in your area" — lifted caller-to-customer conversion 16% with zero change in substance.
*   **Scarcity:** Loss framing beats gain framing ("If you don't act, you lose $20,000" vs. "If you act, you gain $20,000"). Purchase limits **more than doubled** sales across seven product categories. Pre-suade the **constraint** before the offer.
*   **Consistency:** Engineer **micro-yes commitments** in the moment of consent. The blood drive wording change — from "We'll mark you down as coming. Thank you!" to "**We'll mark you down as coming, okay?** [pause]" — moved participation from 70% to **82.4%.** Use spoken confirmations, typed names, checkbox commitments, and the sign-at-the-top honesty pledge. **And sometimes you need only remind them** of a commitment they already hold — as in the marriage-equality campaign that saturated Justice Kennedy's environment with **his own prior words** ("human dignity," "individual liberty," "personal freedoms/rights") for months, then used the same words in court, and won 5–4.
*   **Unity (the seventh):** The most powerful and least used. Move from "like us" to **"of us."**
    *   **Being together:** shared **kinship** (family imagery, "brothers," "community," "our people"), shared **place** (home, locality, region — home-state university, local casualty, same unit). The 97% survey return (one point for a parent's response) is the purest evidence of how much kinship outruns every incentive you can buy.
    *   **Acting together:** synchrony, shared ritual, music, co-creation, reciprocal exchange.
    *   **The single best unity play in marketing:** ask for **advice**, not opinions or expectations. Advice-givers reported wanting to patronize the brand significantly more — because advice puts a person in a **merging** state of mind, while opinions put them in an **introspective** one. *"When we ask for advice, we are usually looking for an accomplice."* Ask customers, colleagues, and even your boss for advice.

### 6. THE STAGE MODEL — WHICH PRINCIPLE, WHEN (Neidert's Core Motives)
*   **Stage 1 — Cultivate a positive association** → **Reciprocity + Liking.** Give first (meaningful, unexpected, customized), highlight real commonalities, offer true compliments. *Goal: rapport.*
*   **Stage 2 — Reduce uncertainty** → **Social Proof + Authority.** Show that peers and experts regard the choice as wise. *Goal: confidence.*
*   **Stage 3 — Motivate action** → **Consistency + Scarcity.** Remind them of what they've said publicly about the problem and the unique enjoyments they'd lose. *Goal: the click.*

### 7. ETHICS — THE PRE-PRE-SUASIVE DECISION (and why it's commercially correct)
*   **The position:** Just because you can steer the moment doesn't mean you may. The tactics are available for good and ill — to **inform** and thereby enhance, or to **fool** and thereby exploit.
*   **The real reason to stay clean (beyond reputation):** The **Triple Tumor** — unethical outward dealing reliably produces **poor employee performance, high turnover, and internal fraud.** The data: deceptive culture lowered individual critical-reasoning performance **20%** and made people stop working sooner; unethical-team members were **80% likely to want to leave** vs. 51%; and the people who stayed in a dishonest unit **cheated 77% more than average** — against their own colleagues and the firm. *"Those who cheat for you will cheat against you."*
*   **The three structural recommendations for an honest organization** (which any serious agency should hold itself and its clients to): include **customer honesty ratings** in staff incentives; measure and include **company ethical reputation** in yearly performance; include **employee ratings of the firm's ethical orientation** in senior management and CEO compensation. The side benefit is a permanent, heightened attentional focus on ethics — which (per Ch. 3–4) elevates both its perceived importance and its causal role.
*   **The Nudge-Too-Far Warning (a marketing tactic, not just an ethics note):** Conspicuous persuasion attempts get discounted. In the product placement study, the most **prominent** placements produced the most recall and the **least** purchase preference — subtle placements were chosen by **47%**, the most obvious by only **27%.** When the trick announces itself, the audience corrects against it. **Keep your devices below the noticing threshold, or you trigger the correction.**

### 8. POST-SUASION — MAKING THE CHANGE STICK
*   Pre-suasion buys the moment. **Durability requires the customer to act.**
*   **Install commitments.** Ask the new customer to **do something active** in the moment of consent — fill in the card themselves, type their own goal, make the referral, book the next call. The medical no-show drop from 3.5% (reminder) to 18% (patient fills in their own card) is the entire argument.
*   **Engineer the cue environment so the preference re-fires automatically.** If you want them to write, surround them with the audience's faces. If you want achievement orientation, the runner. If you want analytical orientation, The Thinker. And in your own work: **change the desktop wallpaper** to match the task you're about to do.
*   **Write if/when-then plans into your onboarding and your own workflow.** *"When it's 8am and I've finished brushing my teeth, then I'll…"* 55% → 79% on medication adherence; 0% → 80% on the drug-addicts' résumé task. **This is the highest-leverage, lowest-cost behavior-change tool in the book.**
*   **Set a recurring cue site.** The lasting version of pre-suasion is not a clever opener before one ask — it is a **regularly encountered cue** in a place you already go, that automatically sends you in the direction you want.

### 9. THE CUSTOMER-DEFENSE CHECKLIST (the same book, read defensively)
*   **Watch for single-chute questions.** If someone asks you only "Are you unhappy with X?" or only "Are you adventurous?", recognize the chute. Demand two-sided wording.
*   **Notice the weather, the mood, the music, and the wallpaper.** You don't need to resist them — you need to **notice** them. In the studies, simply being reminded of the weather ("How's the weather over there?") was enough to eliminate the bias; asking sports fans about the game before the politician eliminated the incumbency effect. **Remind = re-mind.**
*   **The trigger list:** a slogan that rhymes, a name like yours, an ad featuring a beautiful vista, a stock symbol that's easy to pronounce, an unfinished story, a mystery designed to hold you, prominent placements that seem too eager. Each of these is a **bias cue** that can be corrected once noticed.
*   **The deliberation gap:** understand why you are vulnerable. Noticing requires time, energy, and motivation — and you are increasingly rushed, overloaded, preoccupied, indifferent, stressed, distracted, tired, and device-interrupted. **The cavalry of deep analysis will rarely arrive because it will rarely be summoned.** Guard the moments that matter (large purchases, contracts, hires) by deliberately creating the time and energy the modern environment removes.
*   **The single best counter-question in the book — Gregory Ratoff's "nonsense":** *"Let me ask you a question, for your information."* A well-posed question causes the recipient to bring to mind a piece of knowledge they already possess but which is not high in consciousness — **and once made focal, it changes everything.** Deploy it on yourself before any important decision, and deploy it on prospects before every offer.
